## The net worth formula

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

- **Assets** are things you own that have monetary value.
- **Liabilities** are debts you owe.

## Step 1: List your assets

Use current values, and be realistic.

### Cash and savings

- Checking accounts
- Savings and money market accounts
- Certificates of deposit

### Investments and retirement

- 401(k), 403(b) and similar plans
- IRAs
- Brokerage accounts
- HSAs

Retirement accounts may be subject to taxes and penalties if withdrawn early. Some people list them at full value, and others reduce them for estimated taxes. Choose one approach and stay consistent.

### Property

- **Home:** a realistic market value, not what you hope it's worth
- **Vehicles:** what you could actually sell them for today
- **Other valuable items** that you could realistically sell, such as equipment

Leave out everyday belongings like clothes and most furniture, which are hard to sell for much.

## Step 2: List your liabilities

Use current balances or payoff amounts:

- Credit cards
- Student loans
- Car loans
- Mortgage and home equity loans
- Personal loans
- Medical debt
- Buy now, pay later plans
- Money owed to family or friends
- Taxes owed

## Step 3: Subtract

### Worked example

| Assets | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Checking | $1,500 |
| Savings | $2,000 |
| Retirement account | $6,500 |
| Car | $12,000 |
| **Total assets** | **$22,000** |

| Liabilities | Balance |
| --- | --- |
| Student loans | $30,000 |
| Credit cards | $4,200 |
| Car loan | $9,800 |
| **Total liabilities** | **$44,000** |

**Net worth = $22,000 − $44,000 = −$22,000**

## What a negative net worth means

A negative net worth means you owe more than you own. It's not a judgment on you as a person. It's a starting point. It's especially common:

- After college, with student loans and few assets
- After buying a new car with a large loan
- After a job loss, medical event or divorce

What matters most is the **direction**. Each month you pay down debt or save, your net worth goes up.

## How paying off debt changes net worth

Paying down debt raises net worth dollar for dollar, as long as the money didn't come from another asset.

- Paying **$500 of credit card debt from your paycheck** raises net worth by $500.
- Moving **$500 from savings to pay a card** keeps net worth the same: assets fall by $500 and liabilities fall by $500. But it may still be smart, since it stops interest charges.

That's why both cutting expenses and increasing income matter. See [cut expenses to pay off debt](cut-expenses-to-pay-off-debt.html) and [side income to pay off debt](side-income-to-pay-off-debt.html).

## How often to track net worth

- **Monthly** if you're in an active payoff or savings push and enjoy seeing progress
- **Quarterly or twice a year** for most people
- **At least yearly** as a financial checkup

Use a simple spreadsheet with one column per date. Track **total assets, total liabilities and net worth**. A chart of the trend can be very motivating.

## Net worth vs. income

High income doesn't guarantee high net worth. Someone earning a lot but spending it all, with large loans, may have a lower net worth than someone with modest income who saves consistently. Net worth reflects **what you keep**, not what you earn.

## Common mistakes

- **Overvaluing** your home, car or possessions
- **Forgetting** smaller debts like store cards, medical bills or loans from family
- **Counting** future income or expected inheritances
- **Comparing** yourself with others instead of your past self
- **Getting discouraged** by a negative number

## Ways to grow your net worth

1. **Pay down high-interest debt.** See the [debt avalanche](debt-avalanche-method.html).
2. **Build an emergency fund.** See [how much emergency fund](how-much-emergency-fund.html).
3. **Invest for retirement**, especially to get any employer match. See [pay off debt or invest](pay-off-debt-or-invest.html).
4. **Avoid new debt** for things that lose value quickly
5. **Increase income** and save much of every raise
6. **Keep your lifestyle steady** as your income grows

## Net worth and debt-to-income ratio

Net worth is a snapshot of what you own and owe. **Debt-to-income ratio** compares monthly debt payments with monthly gross income. Lenders use it to judge how easily you can handle new payments. Both are useful. See [how much debt is too much](how-much-debt-is-too-much.html).

## Net worth and generosity

For people of faith, net worth is a tool, not a scoreboard. Romans 13:8 says, *"Let no debt remain outstanding, except the continuing debt to love one another."* Tracking net worth helps you see progress toward that freedom, and makes room to give more as debt falls.

Q: How do I calculate my net worth?
A: Add up the value of everything you own, including cash, investments and property, then subtract everything you owe. The result is your net worth.
Q: Is it normal to have a negative net worth?
A: Yes, especially early in your career or with student loans. What matters is the direction. Paying down debt and saving raise your net worth over time.
Q: Should I include my home in my net worth?
A: Yes, at a realistic market value, and include your mortgage as a liability. Some people also track net worth without their home to focus on liquid assets.
Q: Does my car count as an asset?
A: Yes, at what you could sell it for today. Include any car loan as a liability. Cars usually lose value over time.
