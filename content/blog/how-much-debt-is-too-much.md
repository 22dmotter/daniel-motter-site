## The quick number: debt-to-income ratio

Your **debt-to-income ratio (DTI)** is the percentage of your gross (before-tax) monthly income that goes to required debt payments. Lenders use it to judge how much more you can safely borrow. You can use it to judge how much pressure you're under.

**DTI = total monthly minimum debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

Include minimum payments on credit cards, car loans, student loans, personal loans, buy now, pay later plans, and your mortgage or rent if you want a full picture. (Lenders often calculate one ratio with housing and one without.)

### Worked example

Say you earn **$5,000 a month** before taxes and your required payments are:

| Debt | Monthly payment |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | $1,100 |
| Car loan | $380 |
| Student loan | $170 |
| Credit card minimums | $100 |
| **Total** | **$1,750** |

$1,750 ÷ $5,000 = **35% DTI**. Without rent, the non-housing debt ratio is $650 ÷ $5,000 = 13%.

### How lenders tend to read DTI

Lenders set their own limits, and they vary by loan type and program. As a rough personal guide:

- **Lower is better.** At a low DTI you can absorb surprises, save and give.
- **The mid-30s and above** usually feels tight. A car repair or a missed shift can push you onto credit cards.
- **Around the low-40s and up**, many lenders become hesitant to approve new credit, and most households feel real strain.

The point isn't to hit a magic number. It's to see how much of every paycheck is already spoken for.

## Warning signs that don't need a calculator

Ratios are helpful, but your daily life often tells you first. You may have too much debt if:

- You only ever make **minimum payments** on your cards.
- Your balances **stay the same or grow** even though you pay every month.
- You use one card, a cash advance or buy now, pay later to **cover another bill**.
- You have **nothing saved** for a $500 surprise.
- You **avoid opening statements** or don't know your total balance.
- Money is a regular source of **arguments or sleepless nights**.
- A **lender has denied you** because of your existing debt load.

If two or three of those sound familiar, it's time for a plan, no matter what your DTI says.

## Another lens: total debt vs. income

DTI measures *payments*, which can hide a big balance stretched over a long term. It also helps to compare your **total non-mortgage debt to your annual take-home pay**. When the debt is larger than what you bring home in a year, it can take several years to clear even with focused effort.

## What to do if you're over the line

1. **List every debt** with balance, APR and minimum payment. Your free credit reports help you find anything you forgot. See [how to check your credit report](check-credit-report-and-dispute-errors.html).
2. **Stop adding new debt.** Take cards out of your wallet and delete saved cards from shopping apps.
3. **Build a small cash cushion** (many people start with around $1,000) so surprises don't go on a card. More in [how much emergency fund you need](how-much-emergency-fund.html).
4. **Make a written budget.** A [zero-based budget](zero-based-budget.html) shows you exactly how much can go to debt each month.
5. **Choose a payoff method.** The [debt snowball](debt-snowball-method.html) builds momentum. The [debt avalanche](debt-avalanche-method.html) minimizes interest.
6. **Lower your interest where you can.** Call to [negotiate a lower rate](negotiate-lower-interest-rate.html) or consider a [balance transfer](balance-transfer-cards.html) if your credit qualifies.
7. **Get help early if payments are unmanageable.** A nonprofit credit counselor can review options like a [debt management plan](credit-counseling-debt-management-plan.html).

## How DTI improves as you pay down debt

Every debt you pay off removes a minimum payment, and your DTI falls immediately. In the example above, paying off the $380 car loan drops total payments to $1,370, or **27.4% DTI**. That freed-up $380 can then be rolled into the next debt. That's the engine behind both the snowball and the avalanche.

Use the [payment calculator](../calculate-your-payment.html) to see how much faster each debt disappears when you add the money freed up by the last one.

It's also worth re-checking your ratio every few months. Income changes, minimums shrink as balances fall, and new obligations creep in. A quick recalculation tells you whether you're moving in the right direction and when you'll have room for bigger goals, like a fuller emergency fund or retirement savings.

@faq
Q: What is a good debt-to-income ratio?
A: Lower is always safer. Many households feel comfortable when total debt payments, including housing, take well under a third of gross income. Lenders publish their own limits, which vary by loan program.
Q: Does DTI affect my credit score?
A: No. Your income isn't on your credit report, so DTI is not part of your credit score. Lenders calculate it separately when you apply for credit.
Q: Should I include rent in my debt-to-income ratio?
A: Mortgage lenders include your housing payment in the "back-end" ratio. For your own planning, include rent too, because it's a fixed obligation that competes with debt payments.
Q: Is $20,000 in debt a lot?
A: It depends on your income, interest rates and the type of debt. $20,000 of credit card debt at a high APR is an emergency for most people. The same amount on a low-rate car loan with a stable income is more manageable. Look at payments, rates and how long payoff will take.
