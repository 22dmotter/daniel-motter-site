## Simple vs. compound interest

**Simple interest** is calculated only on the original amount (the principal). **Compound interest** is calculated on the principal *plus* any interest already added.

Example with $1,000 at 10% a year:

| Year | Simple interest balance | Compound interest balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $1,100 | $1,100 |
| 2 | $1,200 | $1,210 |
| 3 | $1,300 | $1,331 |
| 10 | $2,000 | About $2,594 |

The gap is small at first and grows wider every year. That's the compounding effect.

## The formula, without the fear

The basic formula is:

**Future value = Principal × (1 + rate ÷ n)^(n × years)**

where *n* is the number of times interest compounds per year. You don't need to memorize it. Just remember that compounding depends on three things:

1. **The rate:** higher grows faster
2. **Time:** more years means dramatically more growth
3. **Frequency:** more frequent compounding grows slightly faster

## Compounding on your side: saving and investing

### A lump sum

$5,000 invested at an *assumed* 7% average annual return:

| Years | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | About $9,836 |
| 20 | About $19,348 |
| 30 | About $38,061 |

Notice how the last 10 years add almost as much as the first 20 combined. Time is the secret ingredient.

### Monthly contributions

Investing **$200 a month** for **30 years** at an assumed 7% compounded monthly grows to about **$243,994**. You'd have contributed **$72,000**. The rest, about **$172,000**, is compound growth. Real investment returns vary and aren't guaranteed, but the principle holds: start early and be consistent.

## Compounding against you: debt

The same force works in reverse when you borrow.

- A credit card at **24% APR** compounded monthly has an effective annual cost of about **26.8%**.
- If you make no payments, a balance at 22% roughly **doubles in about 3.3 years** (by the Rule of 72 below).
- Minimum payments barely outpace the interest, which is why balances can linger for years. See [what happens if you only pay the minimum](minimum-payment-only.html).

Most installment loans, like car loans and mortgages, charge simple interest on the remaining balance each month, so they don't compound unpaid interest the way cards can. But stretched-out terms still mean lots of total interest.

## The Rule of 72

Divide **72** by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double.

| Rate | Years to double (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| 4% | 18 |
| 7% | 10.3 |
| 10% | 7.2 |
| 22% | 3.3 |

It's a quick way to feel the difference between a savings rate and a credit card rate.

## Compounding frequency in practice

How often interest compounds makes a small but real difference. $10,000 at 6% for one year grows to:

| Compounding | Value after 1 year |
| --- | --- |
| Annually | $10,600.00 |
| Monthly | About $10,616.78 |
| Daily | About $10,618.31 |

The difference between monthly and daily is tiny. The rate and the number of years matter far more than the frequency. When comparing savings accounts, the APY already includes compounding, so you can compare APYs directly.

## Why starting early beats saving more later

Imagine two savers who both earn an assumed 7% a year. One invests $200 a month from age 25 to 35, then stops. The other starts at 35 and invests $200 a month until 65. The early saver contributes only **$24,000** over 10 years. The late saver contributes **$72,000** over 30 years. Yet at 65, the early saver ends up with about **$280,968** and the late saver with about **$243,994** (assuming monthly compounding at a steady 7%, which real markets never deliver exactly). The early dollars simply had more time to grow. That's why even small amounts in your twenties and thirties matter. The same logic is why paying off high-interest debt early is so valuable: every year you carry it, it compounds against you.

## How to put compounding on your side

1. **Pay off high-interest debt first.** You stop compounding against yourself. See [how to pay off credit card debt](how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt.html).
2. **Pay credit card statements in full** once debt-free to avoid interest entirely.
3. **Start saving early**, even small amounts. Time does the heavy lifting.
4. **Automate contributions** so growth happens without relying on willpower.
5. **Reinvest earnings** rather than spending them.
6. **Keep fees low.** Investment fees compound too, reducing your returns.

@faq
Q: How often does interest compound?
A: It depends on the account. Savings accounts often compound daily or monthly. Credit cards usually compute interest daily and add it monthly. Many loans charge simple interest monthly on the remaining balance.
Q: Is compound interest good or bad?
A: Both. It's good when it grows your savings and investments, and bad when it increases what you owe on debt like credit cards.
Q: What is the Rule of 72?
A: Divide 72 by an interest rate to estimate how many years it takes for money to double at that rate. At 8%, it's about 9 years.
Q: Do student loans compound?
A: Federal student loans generally accrue simple interest, but unpaid interest can be capitalized, meaning added to principal, in certain situations, after which you pay interest on it. Check your loan terms and servicer for details.
