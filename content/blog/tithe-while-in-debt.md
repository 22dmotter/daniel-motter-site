## Why this question matters

For many people of faith, giving isn't just a budget line. It's an act of worship and trust. At the same time, debt can feel like a weight you want to remove as fast as possible. When money is tight, it's natural to wonder which comes first.

This site's foundation is Romans 13:8: *"Let no debt remain outstanding, except the continuing debt to love one another."* It's a reminder that getting out of debt and loving others aren't opposites. Both can shape how you handle money.

## Common views

Christians hold a range of thoughtful positions. Here are three common ones, described without taking sides.

### 1. Give first, always

Some believe giving should come off the top of every paycheck, before debt payments or savings, regardless of circumstances. For them, a tithe, often understood as 10%, is a fixed commitment, and the rest of the budget is built around it.

### 2. Give, but adjust the amount for a season

Others keep giving consistently but at a lower amount while in serious debt, then increase it as debts are paid. They see it as a way to keep generosity central while freeing up more cash to become debt-free sooner, which can allow greater giving later.

### 3. Focus on obligations, then give generously

Some believe that paying what you already owe is the priority when you're behind, and that generosity grows as you're able. They emphasize keeping your word to lenders.

Whichever view you hold, it's worth remembering that **paying bills you've agreed to pay** is part of living with integrity. Falling behind on rent or minimum payments so you can give more can create new problems.

## Questions to ask yourself

- **What do I believe about giving?** Think it through and, if helpful, talk with your pastor or a trusted mentor.
- **Am I current on all my bills?** If not, what would it take to get current?
- **If I'm married, do we agree?** Money decisions in marriage go better as a team.
- **What's my motivation?** Guilt and pressure make shaky foundations; conviction and joy last longer.
- **Can I give in other ways too?** Time, skills and hospitality are valuable gifts.

## How to budget giving while paying off debt

A [zero-based budget](zero-based-budget.html) works well because every dollar gets a job, including giving.

### Example on $4,000 a month

| Approach | Giving | Extra debt payment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tithe 10% of take-home pay | $400 | $400 |
| Give 5% for a season | $200 | $600 |
| Give 2% for a season | $80 | $720 |

These figures assume $800 a month is left for giving and extra debt payments combined, after essentials, minimum payments and savings. Percentages are of $4,000 take-home pay. Adjust the numbers to your situation. The point is to **decide on purpose** instead of giving whatever is left, or nothing at all.

### A tithe on gross or net income?

People differ on this too. Some give based on gross income, before taxes, and others on take-home pay. Pick an approach you're at peace with and be consistent.

## Giving without overspending

- **Make giving a line item**, not an impulse
- **Automate it** like any other priority
- **Avoid giving with a credit card** you can't pay off in full. Adding interest to a gift doesn't help anyone.
- **Track it** so you can see how generosity and payoff are both progressing
- **Plan for special giving**, such as a mission trip or a friend in need, with a [sinking fund](sinking-funds.html)

## Generosity grows as debt shrinks

One of the most motivating parts of a debt-free plan is what comes after. Every payment you no longer owe can be redirected. Many people find they can give far more once their minimum payments are gone.

## Non-financial ways to give

Generosity isn't only about money. While budgets are tight, you can:

- **Volunteer** at church or in your community
- **Use your skills** to help someone, such as fixing a car or helping with a resume
- **Show hospitality** by sharing a meal
- **Encourage** someone else working through debt

## Talk it over

If you're unsure what to do, talk with your pastor, a trusted mentor or a financial counselor who respects your values. They can help you find a plan that fits your faith and your finances.
