## Step 1: Don't panic, and don't ignore it

Medical bills often arrive before insurance has finished processing, and they can contain errors. Ignoring a bill can lead to collections, but paying too fast can mean paying for mistakes. Take a few deliberate steps first.

## Step 2: Get an itemized bill

Ask the provider for an **itemized bill** listing every charge with billing codes. Look for:

- Duplicate charges
- Services you didn't receive
- Wrong dates or quantities
- Charges for canceled tests or procedures

Compare it with your **Explanation of Benefits (EOB)** from your insurer. If something doesn't match, call the billing office and ask for a correction.

## Step 3: Check insurance

- Was the claim submitted to the right insurer with the correct information?
- Was anything **denied**? You can often **appeal** a denial with the insurer.
- Was the provider in network? If you got an unexpected out-of-network bill for emergency care or certain care at an in-network facility, the federal **No Surprises Act** may protect you. Learn more at [cms.gov/nosurprises](https://www.cms.gov/nosurprises).
- If you were uninsured or self-pay, you may have been entitled to a **good-faith estimate**. If the bill is substantially higher than the estimate, you may be able to dispute it.

## Step 4: Ask for financial assistance

Many hospitals have **financial assistance policies**, sometimes called charity care. Nonprofit hospitals are generally required by federal tax rules to have written financial assistance policies and to limit what they charge eligible patients. Ask:

- "Do you have a financial assistance program?"
- "What are the income limits, and how do I apply?"
- "Can you pause collections while my application is reviewed?"

Assistance can reduce or even eliminate a bill, depending on your income. Apply even if you think you might not qualify.

## Step 5: Negotiate the bill

If you don't qualify for assistance:

- **Ask for a discount** for paying a lump sum, or for the self-pay rate
- **Ask for an interest-free payment plan** with an affordable monthly amount
- **Get any agreement in writing**

Many providers offer payment plans directly. Choose one with a payment you can reliably make.

## Be careful with medical credit cards and loans

Providers sometimes offer **medical credit cards** or financing. Some have **deferred interest**: if you don't pay the full balance by the end of the promotion, interest may be charged back to the original purchase date. A direct, interest-free payment plan with the provider is often safer.

Also think twice before putting a large medical bill on a regular credit card. You'd likely lose the chance to negotiate or get assistance, and you'd start paying high interest.

## How medical debt affects your credit

According to the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/), the three nationwide credit bureaus:

- **Don't report paid medical collections**
- **Don't report medical collections under $500**
- **Wait one year** before reporting unpaid medical collections, giving you time to resolve insurance or payment issues

A CFPB rule that would have removed most medical debt from credit reports was **vacated by a federal court on July 11, 2025**, so larger unpaid medical collections can still appear after a year. Some states have their own protections.

Scoring models also treat medical collections differently. Some newer models, like FICO 9 and VantageScore 3.0 and 4.0, weigh medical collections less than other collections.

If you see medical debt on your report that should've been removed or that you've paid, **dispute it**. See [how to check your credit report and dispute errors](check-credit-report-and-dispute-errors.html).

## If your bill goes to collections

- **Request validation** of the debt
- **Check for errors** against your itemized bill and insurance records
- **Ask the original provider** whether financial assistance can still apply
- **Know your rights** under federal law. See [debt collectors: your rights](debt-collectors-your-rights.html).

## How to prioritize medical debt with other debts

Medical debt usually doesn't carry interest if you're on a provider payment plan, and it's treated more leniently on credit reports than many other debts. Priorities generally look like this:

1. **Essentials:** housing, utilities, food, transportation
2. **Secured debts** like a mortgage or car loan
3. **Minimum payments on high-interest debt**
4. **Affordable medical payment plans**

Don't take high-interest debt to pay off interest-free medical debt without carefully comparing the costs.

## When medical debt is overwhelming

If medical bills are part of a larger debt problem:

- Talk to a **nonprofit credit counselor**. See [credit counseling and debt management plans](credit-counseling-debt-management-plan.html).
- Ask about **hospital patient advocates** or financial counselors
- Consider **legal aid** if you're being sued
- In serious cases, **bankruptcy** can discharge medical debt. See [debt settlement vs. bankruptcy](debt-settlement-vs-bankruptcy.html).

## Preventing future medical debt

- Know your **deductible and out-of-pocket maximum**
- Use **in-network** providers when possible
- Ask for **cost estimates** before non-emergency care
- Build an **emergency fund** and a health sinking fund. See [sinking funds](sinking-funds.html).
- Use an **HSA or FSA** if available

## You're not alone

Medical bills can come from circumstances no one plans for. Taking these steps can lower what you owe and protect your credit while you get back on your feet.

Q: Does medical debt go on your credit report?
A: The three credit bureaus don't report paid medical collections or medical collections under $500, and they wait a year before reporting unpaid ones. Larger unpaid medical collections can still appear after that.
Q: Can I negotiate a medical bill?
A: Yes. Ask for an itemized bill, check for errors, ask about financial assistance, and request a discount or interest-free payment plan. Get agreements in writing.
Q: Should I put medical bills on a credit card?
A: Usually not. You may lose the chance for assistance or an interest-free plan and start paying high interest. A direct payment plan with the provider is often better.
