## How the debt snowball works

The method is simple enough to fit on an index card:

1. **List your debts from smallest balance to largest**, ignoring interest rates. (Mortgages are usually left out.)
2. **Pay the minimum on everything** so you stay current.
3. **Put every extra dollar toward the smallest balance** until it's paid off.
4. **Roll that payment forward.** Add the old payment to the minimum of the next-smallest debt.
5. **Repeat** until every balance is zero.

The "snowball" is your payment. It starts small, and each debt you knock out makes it bigger.

## Why paying the smallest balance first can work

On paper, ignoring interest rates seems odd. But debt payoff is a behavior problem as much as a math problem. Most people don't quit because the math was wrong. They quit because months go by and nothing feels different.

The snowball gives you **a visible win early**. Closing out an account in the first few months proves the plan works. It also **cuts the number of bills** you juggle and **frees up a full minimum payment** that you redirect immediately.

## A worked example

Suppose you have three debts and can put **$500 a month** total toward them.

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Card A | $900 | 18% | $30 |
| Personal loan | $3,200 | 11% | $105 |
| Card B | $6,500 | 27% | $160 |

Minimums add up to $295, so there's **$205 extra** each month.

**Snowball order (smallest first):** Card A → Personal loan → Card B.

- **Months 1–4:** Card A gets $30 + $205 = $235 a month and is **paid off in month 4**.
- **Months 5–13:** The $235 rolls into the personal loan, which now gets $105 + $235 = $340 a month. It's **gone by month 13**.
- **Months 14–28:** All $500 hits Card B, which is **paid off in month 28**.

Total interest paid: about **$3,328**. Debt-free in **28 months**, with the first account closed in four.

> Using the same numbers, the debt avalanche (highest rate first) finishes in about 27 months with about $2,666 of interest, which is roughly $660 less. That's the trade-off: the snowball buys earlier wins at the cost of some interest. See [snowball vs. avalanche](debt-snowball-vs-avalanche.html) for the full comparison.

These figures assume rates and minimums stay fixed, interest is charged monthly, and no new charges are added. Run your own numbers in the [payoff planner](../calculate-your-payment.html), which supports both methods.

## Pros and cons

### Pros

- **Fast early wins** that build momentum and confidence
- **Fewer bills** to track as small debts disappear
- **Simple rule** that's easy to follow and explain to a spouse
- **Built-in acceleration** as freed-up payments roll forward

### Cons

- **Usually more total interest** than the avalanche, sometimes a lot more if your biggest balance carries your highest rate
- **The big, expensive debt waits**, so it keeps growing interest in the meantime
- **Doesn't work if you keep borrowing**, for any method

## When the snowball is a great fit

- You've tried to pay off debt before and lost steam.
- Your balances are fairly close in interest rate, so the cost difference is small.
- You have several small debts you could clear quickly.
- You and your partner need a simple plan you can both stick to.

If your largest debt also has a much higher rate than the others, the avalanche's savings may be worth the slower start. Or try a hybrid: knock out one or two tiny balances for quick wins, then switch to highest rate first.

## Tips to keep the snowball rolling

- **Automate every minimum** so a missed payment never costs you a late fee or a penalty APR.
- **Pay the extra right after payday.** Money left sitting in checking tends to get spent.
- **Throw windfalls at the target.** Tax refunds, bonuses, gifts and side income can knock months off.
- **Celebrate small, cheaply.** Mark each payoff, then go right back to work.
- **Track it publicly or with someone.** I post my own recorded contributions on my [daily contributions page](../daily-contributions.html). Accountability helps.
- **Keep a small emergency fund** so surprises don't go back on a card. See [how much emergency fund you need](how-much-emergency-fund.html).

## What to do with the snowball once you're debt-free

When the last debt is gone, the payment you built doesn't need to disappear. Point it at a full emergency fund, retirement contributions and generous giving. That same habit, spending less than you earn and directing the difference on purpose, is how you [stay out of debt](how-to-stay-out-of-debt.html) for good.

Q: Does the debt snowball include my mortgage?
A: Most people leave the mortgage out and focus on consumer debts like credit cards, car loans, personal loans and student loans. Paying the mortgage early can be a later goal once everything else is gone.
Q: What if two debts have similar balances?
A: Put the one with the higher interest rate first. You'll get a similar quick win and save a little interest.
Q: Is the debt snowball better than the avalanche?
A: Mathematically, the avalanche usually costs less. The snowball often works better for people who need early wins to stay motivated. The best method is the one you will actually follow to the end.
Q: Should I stop retirement contributions to do the debt snowball?
A: That depends on your situation. Many people keep at least any employer match, because it's an immediate return. Others pause briefly to clear high-interest debt faster. Weigh the interest rates and your timeline, and consider professional advice if you're unsure.
Q: Can I use the snowball with student loans?
A: Yes. Federal student loans are listed like any other debt. If you're on an income-driven plan or pursuing forgiveness, check how extra payments affect that program before prepaying.
