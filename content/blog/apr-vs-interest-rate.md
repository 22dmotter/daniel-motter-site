## Interest rate: the base price of borrowing

The **interest rate** is the percentage the lender charges on the money you borrow. It determines how much interest is added to your balance and, on installment loans, your monthly payment.

## APR: the broader cost

The **APR** takes the interest rate and adds in certain required fees, such as loan origination fees or some mortgage closing costs, then expresses the total as a yearly rate. Federal Truth in Lending rules require lenders to disclose APR so borrowers can compare offers on a more equal footing.

## Worked example: how a fee raises the APR

Two lenders offer a **$10,000 personal loan for 5 years at a 9% interest rate**. The monthly payment on both is about **$207.58**.

- **Lender A** charges no fees. APR ≈ **9%**.
- **Lender B** charges a **$500 origination fee** taken out of the loan, so you receive $9,500 but repay $10,000 plus interest. APR ≈ **11.2%**.

Same interest rate, same payment, but Lender B's loan is meaningfully more expensive. The APR reveals that.

| | Lender A | Lender B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate | 9% | 9% |
| Fee | $0 | $500 |
| Cash you receive | $10,000 | $9,500 |
| Monthly payment | $207.58 | $207.58 |
| APR | About 9% | About 11.2% |

## APR on credit cards

For credit cards, the APR you see is effectively the interest rate. Cards don't usually roll annual fees into the APR. A card may list several APRs:

- **Purchase APR**
- **Balance transfer APR**, often with an intro rate and a fee
- **Cash advance APR**, usually higher, with no grace period
- **Penalty APR**, triggered by late payments

Card interest is calculated daily from the APR, which is covered in [how credit card interest works](how-credit-card-interest-works.html).

## APR vs. APY

They sound alike but measure different things:

- **APR** is a simple annual rate. It doesn't include the effect of compounding.
- **APY (annual percentage yield)** includes compounding. It's usually used for savings accounts.

A 24% APR compounded monthly works out to an effective annual rate of about **26.8%**. That's why carrying a card balance costs more than the APR alone suggests. When you're saving, look at APY. When you're borrowing, compare APRs and remember compounding works against you. See [what is compound interest](what-is-compound-interest.html).

## Fixed vs. variable APR

- **Fixed APR:** stays the same for the life of the loan (common on personal loans, car loans and federal student loans).
- **Variable APR:** moves with a benchmark rate (common on credit cards, HELOCs and some private loans). If rates rise, your cost rises.

When comparing a fixed and a variable offer, think about what happens if rates climb.

## What APR doesn't tell you

- **Total cost over time.** A longer loan can have a low APR but cost more total interest. Compare the **total of payments** too.
- **Some fees.** Late fees, prepayment penalties and some optional products may not be included.
- **Deferred interest traps** on promotional offers.

## How to compare loan offers

1. Get APRs for the **same loan amount and term** from each lender.
2. Compare the **monthly payment** and the **total amount repaid**.
3. Check for **origination fees, prepayment penalties** and whether the rate is fixed or variable.
4. Prequalify with a soft credit check where possible before applying.
5. Choose the offer with the lowest total cost that has a payment you can comfortably afford.

## APR on mortgages and car loans

On a **mortgage**, the APR includes the interest rate plus certain lender charges, such as origination fees and discount points. That's why a loan with a lower interest rate but expensive points can have a higher APR than a no-points loan. If you plan to sell or refinance within a few years, paying upfront points may not pay off, because the savings are spread over the full term.

On **car loans**, watch for add-ons rolled into the amount financed, such as extended warranties or service contracts. They raise the amount you borrow and your total interest even if the rate looks competitive. Ask for the out-the-door price and the loan terms separately, and compare the APR and total of payments from your bank or credit union before you accept dealer financing.

## A quick way to estimate interest cost

For a rough monthly interest figure on any balance, multiply the balance by the APR and divide by 12. A $4,000 balance at 18% costs about $60 a month in interest ($4,000 × 18% ÷ 12). That single number often motivates people more than the APR itself.

## Using APR to prioritize your debts

When you have several debts, APR tells you which is most expensive. In the [debt avalanche](debt-avalanche-method.html), you rank debts by APR and attack the highest first. Even with the [snowball](debt-snowball-method.html), knowing your APRs helps you spot when negotiating or refinancing could save money.

Q: Is APR the same as interest rate?
A: Not always. On credit cards they're usually the same. On loans, APR includes certain fees, so it's often higher than the interest rate.
Q: Is a lower APR always better?
A: For the same loan amount and term, a lower APR means a lower cost. But compare the term and total repaid too. A longer loan can have a low APR and still cost more overall.
Q: What is the difference between APR and APY?
A: APR is a yearly rate without compounding. APY includes compounding and is mostly used for savings. When borrowing, compare APRs. When saving, compare APYs.
Q: Why is my APR higher than advertised?
A: Advertised rates are usually for borrowers with the strongest credit. Your APR depends on your credit history, income, the loan amount and term.
