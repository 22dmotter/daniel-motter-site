## What debt consolidation means

Instead of paying several creditors, you pay one. The old debts are paid off by:

- A **personal loan** (debt consolidation loan)
- A **0% balance transfer credit card**
- A **home equity loan or HELOC**
- A **401(k) loan** (generally risky)
- A **debt management plan** through a nonprofit credit counselor, which isn't a loan but combines payments and often lowers rates
- A **federal Direct Consolidation Loan** for federal student loans, which works differently (more below)

Consolidation **doesn't reduce what you owe**. It changes the rate, the payment and the structure.

## When consolidation saves money: an example

You have **$12,000 of credit card debt at an average 23% APR** and you pay $400 a month.

| Option | Payment | Time | Interest and fees |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the cards | $400 | About 46 months | About $6,028 |
| 3-year personal loan at 12% APR, 3% fee | About $411 | 36 months | About $2,792 including the fee |

With about the same monthly payment, the loan saves roughly **$3,236** and finishes **10 months sooner**. The fee assumes you borrow enough to cover it: about $12,371, so $12,000 reaches the cards after a $371 origination fee.

## When consolidation backfires

### Stretching the term

A lower payment can hide a higher total cost. A 6-year loan at a moderate rate can cost more interest than paying the cards aggressively for 3 years. **Compare the total of payments, not just the monthly amount.**

### Running the cards back up

This is the biggest risk. With zero balances, the cards feel like "available money" again. If you add new charges, you'll end up with the loan *and* new card debt. Remove the cards from your wallet and online accounts.

### High fees

Origination fees, balance transfer fees and closing costs eat into savings. Factor them into the APR and total cost. See [APR vs. interest rate](apr-vs-interest-rate.html).

### Turning unsecured debt into secured debt

Using a **home equity loan or HELOC** to pay off credit cards turns debt that can't take your house into debt secured by your house. If you can't pay, your home is at risk. See [using home equity to pay off debt](home-equity-to-pay-off-debt.html).

## Pros and cons at a glance

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| One payment, one due date | Doesn't reduce the principal |
| Potentially lower APR | Fees can reduce savings |
| Fixed payoff date with installment loans | Longer terms can cost more |
| Can improve credit utilization | Hard inquiry and new account |
| Less mental clutter | Easy to run cards back up |

## Is consolidation right for you? A checklist

- Will the **new APR, including fees**, be lower than your current average rate?
- Is the **term the same or shorter** than you'd need to pay off on your own?
- Is the **payment affordable** with room for savings?
- Have you **stopped using** the accounts you're paying off?
- Do you have a **starter emergency fund** so surprises don't go on a card?
- Have you **compared** at least two or three offers?

If you checked every box, consolidation could be a smart move. If not, consider the [snowball](debt-snowball-method.html) or [avalanche](debt-avalanche-method.html), or [ask your issuers for lower rates](negotiate-lower-interest-rate.html).

## Federal student loan consolidation is different

A **Direct Consolidation Loan** combines federal student loans into one federal loan. The new interest rate is a **weighted average** of the old rates, rounded up slightly, so it **doesn't lower your rate**. People consolidate to simplify payments or to make certain older loans eligible for some repayment or forgiveness programs. Under recent law changes, consolidating on or after July 1, 2026 can also limit which repayment plans you can use. Check [studentaid.gov](https://studentaid.gov/) before consolidating. See [student loan repayment plans](student-loan-repayment-plans.html).

Refinancing federal loans into a **private** loan is different again. It can lower your rate but gives up federal protections. See [should I refinance my student loans](refinance-student-loans.html).

## Consolidation vs. settlement

Debt consolidation pays your debts in full with new financing. **Debt settlement** tries to pay less than you owe, usually after accounts go delinquent, and can seriously damage credit. Don't confuse the two. Read [debt settlement vs. bankruptcy](debt-settlement-vs-bankruptcy.html).

## How to consolidate wisely

1. List all debts, rates and payments.
2. Check your credit reports and scores.
3. Prequalify with several lenders using soft credit checks.
4. Compare APRs, fees, terms and total cost.
5. Use the loan proceeds **only** to pay off the debts. Some lenders pay creditors directly.
6. Confirm each old account shows a $0 balance.
7. Put the new payment on autopay and keep the old cards out of reach.

@faq
Q: Does debt consolidation hurt your credit?
A: Applying adds a hard inquiry and a new account, which can lower your score briefly. Paying off cards often lowers utilization, which can help. On-time payments improve your score over time.
Q: What credit score do you need for a debt consolidation loan?
A: Requirements vary by lender. Better credit generally gets better rates. Prequalifying with a soft check shows the rates you may qualify for without hurting your score.
Q: Is debt consolidation the same as debt settlement?
A: No. Consolidation repays your debts in full with a new loan or account. Settlement tries to pay creditors less than the full balance and usually damages your credit.
Q: Can I consolidate debt with bad credit?
A: It's harder and rates may be high. A nonprofit debt management plan may be a better option, because it can lower rates without a new loan.
