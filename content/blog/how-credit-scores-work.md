## Credit reports vs. credit scores

Your **credit reports** are records kept by the three nationwide credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. They list your accounts, balances, payment history, inquiries and certain collections.

Your **credit score** is a number calculated from a report by a scoring model. The most widely used are **FICO** and **VantageScore**, each with multiple versions. That's why you can have many different scores at once. The model, the version and the bureau's data can all differ.

## The five factors in a FICO score

According to [myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score), FICO scores are based on these categories for the general population:

| Factor | Weight | What it looks at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment history | 35% | Whether you pay on time, and any late payments, collections or bankruptcies |
| Amounts owed | 30% | How much you owe, including how much of your credit card limits you're using |
| Length of credit history | 15% | Age of your oldest, newest and average accounts |
| New credit | 10% | Recently opened accounts and hard inquiries |
| Credit mix | 10% | Having different types, such as cards and installment loans |

The weights are general. Your actual score depends on your whole profile.

### 1. Payment history (35%)

The biggest factor. One payment 30 or more days late can hurt your score, and late payments can stay on your report for up to seven years. **Set up autopay** for at least the minimum on every account.

### 2. Amounts owed (30%)

This includes your **credit utilization**: credit card balances divided by limits. Lower is generally better. See [credit utilization explained](credit-utilization-explained.html).

### 3. Length of credit history (15%)

Older accounts help. That's one reason closing your oldest card can hurt. See [should I close a credit card after paying it off](close-credit-card-after-paying-off.html).

### 4. New credit (10%)

Opening several accounts in a short time can lower your score temporarily. Hard inquiries from applications have a small effect that fades. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect it.

### 5. Credit mix (10%)

Having both revolving credit (cards) and installment loans can help slightly. It's not a reason to take on debt you don't need.

## What credit scores don't include

Your score doesn't use your **income, savings, job, age, race, religion, national origin, sex or marital status**. Checking accounts usually don't appear on credit reports, and paying with a debit card doesn't build credit.

## Score ranges

FICO scores commonly range from **300 to 850**. Lenders set their own standards for what counts as good, and those standards differ by loan type. In general, a higher score means more approvals and lower rates.

## How to improve your credit score

1. **Pay every bill on time.** Autopay helps.
2. **Lower your card balances.** Paying down debt reduces utilization. See [how to pay off credit card debt](how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt.html).
3. **Check your reports for errors** and dispute them. See [how to check your credit report](check-credit-report-and-dispute-errors.html).
4. **Keep old accounts open** when it makes sense.
5. **Apply for new credit sparingly.**
6. **Be patient.** Negative items lose impact over time.

## How long does it take?

Some changes show up quickly. Lowering card balances can raise a score as soon as the new balances are reported, often within a month or two. Recovering from late payments, collections or bankruptcy takes longer. See [how to rebuild credit after debt](rebuild-credit-after-debt.html).

## A sample month that helps your score

1. Autopay covers the minimum on every card and loan.
2. You pay most of each card balance before the statement closes.
3. You don't apply for new credit you don't need.
4. You review one free credit report for errors.

None of this requires paying interest or taking on new debt. It just requires consistency.

## Common myths

- **"Checking my score hurts it."** No, checking your own score is a soft inquiry.
- **"Carrying a balance builds credit."** No. Paying in full avoids interest and still shows activity.
- **"I need to be in debt to have good credit."** You need credit accounts with good history, not debt you pay interest on.
- **"Paying off a collection removes it."** Not necessarily. Paid collections may remain on your report, though some newer scoring models ignore paid collections.

@faq
Q: What is the biggest factor in a credit score?
A: According to FICO, payment history is the biggest factor, at about 35% of the score for the general population, followed by amounts owed at about 30%.
Q: Does checking my credit score lower it?
A: No. Checking your own score or report is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect your score. Hard inquiries from credit applications can have a small, temporary effect.
Q: Why do I have different credit scores?
A: There are different scoring models and versions, and each bureau may have slightly different information about you. Lenders may also use industry-specific scores.
Q: Does income affect my credit score?
A: No. Credit scores don't use income, but lenders may consider income separately when you apply for credit.
Q: How fast can I raise my credit score?
A: Lowering credit card balances can help within a month or two once new balances are reported. Recovering from late payments or collections takes longer.
