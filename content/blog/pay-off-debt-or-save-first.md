## The tension between saving and paying debt

Every extra dollar can do one of two jobs: protect you (savings) or free you (debt payoff). Put everything into debt and the next emergency goes back on a card. Put everything into savings and high-interest debt keeps growing. The answer is to do both, in a sensible order.

## A simple order that works for most people

1. **Pay every minimum on time.** This protects your credit and avoids fees.
2. **Save a starter emergency fund.** A common target is around $1,000, or one month of bare-bones expenses if that's bigger for you.
3. **Capture any employer retirement match**, if you have one. It's often an immediate, generous return. See [pay off debt or invest](pay-off-debt-or-invest.html).
4. **Pay off high-interest debt aggressively.** Credit cards, payday loans and other high-rate balances come first.
5. **Build a full emergency fund**, often three to six months of essential expenses. See [how much emergency fund you need](how-much-emergency-fund.html).
6. **Tackle lower-rate debt and long-term goals** together, such as retirement, giving, and car or home savings.

Your situation may call for adjustments, like a bigger cushion if your income is irregular or you're the only earner.

## Why save anything before paying debt?

Because life happens. Without cash, a $600 car repair goes on a credit card at 20% or more. That one emergency can undo months of progress and wreck your motivation. A small cash fund breaks that cycle.

It also changes how emergencies *feel*. When you can cover a surprise in cash, it's an inconvenience, not a crisis.

## Why not save more before paying debt?

Money in savings typically earns far less than your credit card charges. Compare:

| Where $1,000 sits for a year | Approximate effect |
| --- | --- |
| Savings account earning 4% APY | You earn about $40 |
| Credit card balance at 24% APR | You pay about $240 or more in interest |

Paying off the card is like earning a **guaranteed, tax-free 24%** on that money. Very few safe investments come close. That's why, once you have a small cushion, high-interest debt usually gets priority.

## What counts as "high-interest" debt?

There's no official cutoff. A practical way to think about it:

- **Clearly high:** credit cards, store cards, payday loans and many personal loans, often in the high teens or above
- **Middle:** some car loans and private student loans
- **Lower:** most mortgages and many federal student loans

The higher the rate, the more urgent it is to pay it off before building savings beyond your starter fund.

## A worked example

Maria has **$8,000 of credit card debt at 22%**, no savings and **$600 a month** of extra money.

- **Months 1–2:** She saves $600 + $400 to reach a $1,000 starter fund. The other $200 in month 2 goes to her card.
- **From month 3:** All $600 extra goes to her cards on top of her minimums.
- When her cards are paid off, she redirects the full amount, minimums plus extra, into savings until she has three months of expenses.

Two months of saving gave her a safety net without delaying her debt plan much.

## Special cases

### Your income is unpredictable

If you're self-employed, commission-based or work variable hours, keep a larger cushion, perhaps one to two months of expenses, before paying debt aggressively. Irregular income makes emergencies more likely.

### You have a big expense coming

If you know a large bill is coming, like a car registration, insurance premium or medical procedure, save for it separately. [Sinking funds](sinking-funds.html) keep predictable expenses from becoming new debt.

### You're facing job uncertainty

If layoffs are possible, build more savings first. Cash is far more useful than a slightly lower balance if you lose income.

### Your debt has a low fixed rate

If your only debt is a low-rate car loan or mortgage, it's reasonable to build a full emergency fund and save for retirement before paying extra.

## Keep the starter fund for emergencies only

Decide what counts as an emergency before it happens: job loss, essential car repairs, urgent medical costs, a necessary home repair. Holidays, sales and vacations don't count. When you use the fund, pause extra debt payments and refill it first.

## How to save while paying debt

- Keep savings in a **separate account**, ideally at a different bank, so it isn't tempting.
- **Automate a small transfer** each payday.
- Put a **portion of windfalls** into savings and the rest toward debt.
- Use the free [debt tracker](../debt-tracker.html) to watch your debt fall while savings grows.

@faq
Q: How much should I save before paying off debt?
A: Many people start with a starter emergency fund of around $1,000 or one month of essential expenses, then pay down high-interest debt before building a larger fund.
Q: Is it smart to use my savings to pay off credit card debt?
A: Using savings beyond a small emergency cushion to pay off high-interest cards usually saves money. Don't drain savings completely, or the next emergency will go back on a card.
Q: Should I save for retirement while paying off debt?
A: If your employer offers a match, contributing enough to get it is often worthwhile. Beyond that, it depends on your interest rates and goals. Consider talking with a qualified advisor.
Q: Where should I keep my emergency fund?
A: Keep it in an insured, easy-to-access savings account separate from your checking, so it's available quickly but not mixed with everyday spending.
