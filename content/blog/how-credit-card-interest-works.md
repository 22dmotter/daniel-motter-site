## The key terms

- **APR (annual percentage rate):** the yearly interest rate on your card. Cards can have different APRs for purchases, balance transfers and cash advances, plus a penalty APR.
- **Daily periodic rate:** APR ÷ 365 (some issuers use 360). This is the rate actually applied each day.
- **Billing cycle:** the period (often about a month) covered by each statement.
- **Statement balance:** what you owed at the end of the billing cycle.
- **Grace period:** time between the end of the cycle and the due date when you can pay the statement balance in full and avoid interest on purchases.

## How the math works, step by step

Most issuers use the **average daily balance** method:

1. Each day, they record your balance.
2. They add up all those daily balances and divide by the number of days in the cycle.
3. They multiply that average by the daily periodic rate and by the number of days.

### Example

You carry **$3,000** on a card with a **24% APR** for a 30-day cycle.

- Daily rate: 24% ÷ 365 ≈ **0.0658%**
- Daily interest: $3,000 × 0.000658 ≈ **$1.97**
- Interest for 30 days: about **$59.18**

That's nearly $60 a month for the privilege of carrying the balance, and it's charged again next month on whatever remains.

## Why credit card interest compounds

If you don't pay the interest off, it becomes part of your balance, and next month you pay interest on it too. That's **compounding**. Because cards compound daily or monthly, the effective yearly cost is higher than the stated APR:

| Stated APR | Effective annual cost if compounded monthly | If compounded daily |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 24% | About 26.8% | About 27.1% |

Learn more in [what is compound interest](what-is-compound-interest.html).

## The grace period: how to pay zero interest

Most cards offer a grace period on purchases. If you **pay the full statement balance by the due date**, you aren't charged interest on those purchases.

But there's a catch: if you **carry any balance** from the previous month, many cards remove the grace period. New purchases then start accruing interest right away, often until you pay in full for one or two cycles. One more reason to get balances to zero.

Credit card issuers must give you a reasonable amount of time to pay. Under federal rules, statements generally have to be delivered at least 21 days before the payment is due.

## Interest you pay even with a grace period

- **Cash advances** usually have no grace period. Interest starts the day you take the cash, often at a higher APR, plus a cash advance fee.
- **Balance transfers** typically carry their own APR and a fee, unless a promotion applies. See [balance transfer cards](balance-transfer-cards.html).
- **Penalty APR** may apply after a late payment and can be much higher than your regular rate.

## Deferred interest vs. 0% APR

Store cards often advertise "no interest if paid in full in 12 months." That's usually **deferred interest**: interest builds in the background, and if any promo balance remains at the deadline, you may be charged *all* of it back to the purchase date.

A true **0% intro APR** card, by contrast, doesn't charge back interest when the promotion ends. Interest just starts on the remaining balance. Read the terms carefully.

## How payments are applied

Under federal rules, when you pay **more than the minimum**, the excess generally must go to the balance with the **highest APR** first. The minimum itself can be applied to lower-rate balances. This matters if you have both a promotional balance and regular purchases on the same card.

## How to pay less credit card interest

1. **Pay the full statement balance** each month once your debt is gone, to keep the grace period.
2. **Pay more than the minimum.** See [what happens if you only pay the minimum](minimum-payment-only.html).
3. **Pay early or more than once a month** to reduce your average daily balance.
4. **Ask for a lower APR.** See [how to negotiate a lower interest rate](negotiate-lower-interest-rate.html).
5. **Move the balance to a lower rate** with a 0% transfer or a lower-rate loan, then stop using the card.
6. **Avoid cash advances** and late payments that trigger penalty rates.
7. **Use a payoff plan.** See [how to pay off credit card debt](how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt.html).

## Where to find your card's numbers

Your statement lists your APRs, the interest charged this cycle, your minimum payment, and a **minimum payment warning** showing how long payoff would take at the minimum. Your cardholder agreement explains how the issuer calculates the balance and when the grace period applies.

Q: Do you pay interest on a credit card if you pay it off every month?
A: Generally no. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, most cards charge no interest on purchases thanks to the grace period. Cash advances are an exception.
Q: Is credit card interest charged daily or monthly?
A: Most issuers calculate interest daily using a daily periodic rate, then add it to your statement monthly.
Q: Why did I get charged interest after paying my balance?
A: You may have paid the current balance after carrying a balance the previous month, which can trigger "trailing" or residual interest that accrued between your statement date and payment date. Paying in full for another cycle usually stops it.
Q: What is a good credit card APR?
A: Lower is always better. Rates vary with your credit and market conditions. If you carry a balance, any APR is expensive, so the best rate is the one you never pay because you pay in full.
