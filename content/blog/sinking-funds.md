## Why sinking funds matter

Many budget blowups aren't true emergencies. They're **irregular but predictable** expenses:

- Car registration and maintenance
- Annual or semiannual insurance premiums
- Holidays and birthdays
- Back-to-school costs
- Medical deductibles and dental work
- Annual subscriptions and memberships
- Home repairs and appliance replacements
- Travel

If you don't plan for them, they often end up on a credit card. Sinking funds turn one big hit into **small monthly amounts**.

## How to calculate a sinking fund

**Monthly amount = expected cost ÷ months until due**

### Example

| Expense | Annual cost | Months | Monthly amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Car registration | $180 | 12 | $15 |
| Holiday gifts | $600 | 12 | $50 |
| Car repairs and tires | $900 | 12 | $75 |
| Annual insurance premium | $1,200 | 12 | $100 |
| **Total** | **$2,880** | | **$240** |

Setting aside **$240 a month** covers **$2,880 a year** of expenses that might otherwise feel like emergencies.

### If you're starting late

If holiday gifts cost $600 and the holidays are 10 months away, you'd save **$60 a month** instead of $50. Start with what you can, and adjust next year's amount.

## Sinking funds vs. an emergency fund

| Sinking funds | Emergency fund |
| --- | --- |
| For expected, planned expenses | For unexpected, urgent expenses |
| You know roughly when and how much | You don't know when or how much |
| Spent and refilled regularly | Ideally rarely touched |

Keeping them separate protects your emergency fund for true emergencies. See [how much emergency fund](how-much-emergency-fund.html).

## How to set up sinking funds

### Step 1: List your irregular expenses

Go through last year's bank and card statements. Look for anything that didn't happen monthly. You may be surprised how much it adds up to.

### Step 2: Estimate each cost and due date

Use past bills or quotes. When unsure, round up.

### Step 3: Calculate monthly amounts

Divide each cost by the months until it's due.

### Step 4: Choose where to keep the money

Options:

- **One savings account** with a simple spreadsheet tracking each fund's balance
- **Multiple savings accounts or "buckets"**, which many banks offer, labeled by goal
- **Cash envelopes** for small funds, if that works for you

A high-yield savings account works well, since the money is safe and earns some interest.

### Step 5: Automate it

Set an automatic transfer on payday. Include sinking funds as line items in your [zero-based budget](zero-based-budget.html).

### Step 6: Spend from the fund when the bill comes

When the expense arrives, pay it from the matching fund. That's the point. No guilt, no new debt.

## Prioritizing when money is tight

If you can't fund everything at once, start with the expenses that are:

1. **Most certain**, like car registration and insurance
2. **Largest**, where a surprise would hurt most
3. **Soonest**

Add more funds as your budget allows. Even partial funding reduces how much ends up on credit.

## Sinking funds while paying off debt

It might feel like money in a sinking fund should go to debt instead. But without sinking funds, predictable expenses often go on a card, creating **new** debt. Sinking funds protect your payoff progress. Many people keep a small set of essential sinking funds during debt payoff and add more once the debt is gone.

## Sinking fund ideas

- **Car:** maintenance, tires, registration, eventual replacement
- **Home:** repairs, appliances, property taxes if not escrowed
- **Health:** deductibles, glasses, dental
- **Family:** birthdays, holidays, school costs, activities
- **Personal:** clothing, haircuts, hobbies
- **Pets:** vet visits, food in bulk
- **Annual bills:** subscriptions, memberships, insurance
- **Fun:** vacations and travel

## A sample month

Say you get paid on the 1st and 15th. On each payday, $120 moves automatically from checking to savings, split across your car, gifts, insurance and registration funds. When the $180 registration renewal arrives in June, you pay it from the registration fund. When tires cost $640 in September, the car fund covers it. Your regular budget doesn't change, and no new card balance appears. By the end of the year, the money you set aside has already paid for the year's irregular bills.

## Tips for success

- **Round up** estimates to build a cushion
- **Review once a year** and adjust amounts
- **Track balances** for each fund
- **Use leftovers** wisely: roll them into next year or into another goal
- **Name your accounts** to stay motivated, such as "Christmas 2027" or "Next car"

Q: What is a sinking fund?
A: Money you set aside regularly for a specific, expected expense, like car repairs, holidays or annual insurance, so you can pay it in cash when it comes due.
Q: How is a sinking fund different from an emergency fund?
A: Sinking funds are for expected expenses you can plan for. An emergency fund is for unexpected, urgent expenses like job loss or a medical bill.
Q: How do I calculate how much to put in a sinking fund?
A: Divide the expected cost by the number of months until it's due. For example, $600 over 12 months is $50 a month.
Q: Where should I keep sinking funds?
A: Many people use a high-yield savings account, either with separate labeled buckets or a single account tracked with a spreadsheet.
