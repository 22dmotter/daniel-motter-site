## What credit counseling is

Credit counseling is a session, often free, with a trained counselor who reviews:

- Your income and expenses
- Your debts and interest rates
- Your options, from budgeting changes to a debt management plan, settlement or bankruptcy

Reputable agencies are usually **nonprofits** and are often affiliated with national organizations like the **National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC)** or the **Financial Counseling Association of America (FCAA)**. Counselors should explain all of your options, not just sell one product.

## How a debt management plan works

1. **The counselor reviews your situation** and confirms you can afford the plan
2. **The agency contacts your creditors**, usually credit card issuers, and proposes lower interest rates and possibly waived fees
3. **You make one monthly payment** to the agency
4. **The agency pays your creditors** each month
5. **You finish in about three to five years**, paying your balances in full

Creditors aren't required to participate, but many card issuers work with credit counseling agencies.

## A worked example

**Debt:** $15,000 of credit card debt at about **24% APR**, paying **$450 a month**.

| Scenario | Payment | Payoff time | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On your own at 24% | $450 | About 56 months | About $9,966 |
| DMP at 8% | $450 | About 38 months | About $2,020 |

In this example, a lower rate saves about **$7,900** in interest and **18 months**. The rate a creditor accepts varies, and agency fees would add to the cost. These numbers are illustrations, not promises.

## Costs

DMPs usually have:

- A **setup fee**
- A **monthly fee**

Fees are often modest and limited by state law in many places. Agencies may reduce or waive fees if you can't afford them. Ask for every fee in writing before you sign up.

## Pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| Lower interest rates on many cards | Enrolled cards are usually closed |
| One monthly payment | Takes about 3 to 5 years |
| Fees and penalties may be waived | Small fees to the agency |
| You repay in full, so less credit damage than settlement | Missing a payment can end the plan's benefits |
| Structure and accountability | Not all creditors or debt types are included |

## How a DMP affects your credit

- **Closing cards** can raise your credit utilization and reduce available credit at first. See [credit utilization explained](credit-utilization-explained.html).
- Some creditors may note that the account is being paid through a **credit counseling plan**. Under widely used scoring models, that note itself generally doesn't lower scores, but lenders reviewing your report may see it.
- **On-time payments** and falling balances usually help your credit over time.

Compared with **debt settlement**, which often involves missed payments and "settled for less" notations, a DMP is generally much gentler on credit. See [debt settlement vs. bankruptcy](debt-settlement-vs-bankruptcy.html).

## What debts are included

DMPs are mainly for **unsecured debt**, especially credit cards. Some may include medical bills, collections or personal loans. They usually **don't** include mortgages, car loans or federal student loans.

## Who a DMP is right for

A DMP may be a good fit if:

- You have **mostly credit card debt** at high interest rates
- You have **steady income** and can afford a monthly payment
- You could pay off the debt in about **five years** with lower rates
- You're willing to **stop using credit cards** during the plan

It may not fit if your debts are far beyond what you can repay. In that case, the counselor should discuss settlement or bankruptcy.

## How to choose a credit counseling agency

Ask:

- "Are you a nonprofit? What are your accreditations?"
- "What services do you offer besides a DMP?"
- "What are all your fees, in writing?"
- "Are your counselors certified?"
- "How do you protect my personal information?"
- "What happens if I miss a payment?"

Check reviews and your state attorney general's office for complaints.

## Red flags

- **Pressure** to sign up before reviewing your budget
- **Large upfront fees**
- **Promises** to make debt disappear or stop all collection calls
- Telling you to **stop paying your creditors** before a plan is in place
- Not explaining **other options**

## DMP vs. consolidation loan

A DMP doesn't require new credit and may work even if your credit isn't good enough for a low-rate loan. A [debt consolidation loan](debt-consolidation-explained.html) gives you a new loan with a fixed rate and lets you keep your cards open, but it requires qualifying. Compare both.

## Making the plan succeed

1. **Build a budget** you can live with. See [zero-based budget](zero-based-budget.html).
2. **Start a small emergency fund** so surprises don't derail you
3. **Confirm creditors receive payments** by checking statements each month
4. **Don't take on new debt** during the plan
5. **Celebrate milestones** as each account reaches zero

## Structure helps

Having one plan, one payment and a clear end date can make a big debt load feel manageable. Whether you choose a DMP or the snowball on your own, consistency is what gets you to zero.

@faq
Q: Does a debt management plan hurt your credit?
A: Closing enrolled cards can affect utilization at first, but on-time payments and falling balances usually help over time. It's generally much less damaging than debt settlement.
Q: How much does a debt management plan cost?
A: Typically a setup fee and a monthly fee, often modest and limited by state law in many places. Agencies may reduce fees if you can't afford them.
Q: Is credit counseling free?
A: Many nonprofit agencies offer free or low-cost counseling sessions. Fees usually apply if you enroll in a debt management plan.
Q: Can I keep my credit cards on a debt management plan?
A: Usually not. Enrolled cards are generally closed, and you're expected to avoid new credit during the plan.
