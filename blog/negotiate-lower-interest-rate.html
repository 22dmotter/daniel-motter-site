Interest & credit cards

How to negotiate a lower interest rate on your credit card

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Call the number on the back of your card, say you're working to pay off your balance, mention your on-time payment history and any lower-rate offers you've received, and ask directly for a lower APR. If the first person says no, politely ask whether anyone else can review it or whether a hardship program is available. Even a few points helps: $8,000 at 27% paid at $300 a month takes about 42 months, but at 21% it takes about 37 and saves about $1,480.

Why asking works

Card issuers want to keep customers who pay. Losing your account to a competitor's balance transfer costs them money. A small rate reduction can be worth it to them, and it's worth a lot to you. There's no guarantee, but a short phone call costs nothing to try.

How much a lower rate can save

$8,000 balance, $300 a month, no new charges:

APRTime to pay offTotal interest
27%About 42 monthsAbout $4,355
21%About 37 monthsAbout $2,871

A six-point reduction saves about $1,484 and five months of payments.

When to call

The best times to ask are when you have leverage: after six to twelve months of on-time payments, after your credit score has improved, after receiving a competing offer, or when you're about to move the balance elsewhere. If you've just missed a payment, focus on getting current first, and ask about hardship options rather than a standard rate reduction.

Before you call

  • Know your numbers: balance, current APR, how long you've had the account and your payment history.
  • Check your credit: a better score since you opened the card strengthens your case. See how to check your credit report.
  • Gather competing offers: balance transfer or lower-rate offers you've received in the mail or prequalified for online.
  • Pick a calm time when you won't be rushed.

A simple script

"Hi, I've been a customer for [X] years and I've made my payments on time. I'm working hard to pay this balance down, and my current APR of [X]% is making that tough. I've received offers from other cards with lower rates. Can you lower my APR on this account?"

If they offer a reduction, ask:

  • "Is this permanent or temporary? When does it end?"
  • "Does it apply to my existing balance?"
  • "Will this affect anything else on my account?"

Write down the rep's name, the date and the new rate. Check your next statement to confirm it.

If they say no

  1. Ask politely if someone else can review it, such as a supervisor or the retention department.
  2. Ask about hardship programs if you've had a job loss, medical issue or income drop. These may lower your rate or payment temporarily. Ask how they affect your account, since some close or freeze the card.
  3. Ask what would make you eligible, for example six more months of on-time payments, and call back then.
  4. Don't threaten to close the account unless you mean it. Closing can affect your credit utilization.

Other ways to lower your interest

  • Balance transfer to a 0% intro APR card if your credit qualifies. See balance transfer cards.
  • Personal loan at a lower fixed rate. See personal loans for credit card debt.
  • Credit union cards often have lower rates. Federal credit unions are generally subject to an interest rate cap set by their regulator.
  • Debt management plan through a nonprofit credit counselor, which often negotiates lower rates across several cards. See credit counseling.

Negotiating other bills and debts

The same approach works elsewhere:

  • Medical bills: ask about financial assistance, discounts for paying in full, or interest-free payment plans. See medical debt.
  • Collections: you can negotiate a payment plan or settlement. Get any agreement in writing first. See your rights with debt collectors.
  • Insurance, phone and internet: call annually to ask for better rates or loyalty discounts, then send the savings to your debt.

What happens after you get a lower rate

Don't let the savings disappear into everyday spending. Keep your payment the same, so the interest you save goes straight to principal. That's how a lower rate turns into a faster payoff. Track it with the free debt tracker.

Keep a call log

Negotiating works better when you're organized. For each call, write down the date, the person you spoke with, what you asked for and what they offered. If an issuer promises a rate change, ask when it will show on your statement. If it doesn't appear, call back with your notes. A simple log also reminds you when to try again, such as six months after a "no."

Common mistakes

  • Not asking at all because it feels awkward
  • Asking once and giving up instead of trying again after a few months
  • Lowering the payment when the rate drops, instead of keeping it the same
  • Closing the card in frustration and raising your utilization
  • Missing a payment after getting a reduction, which can trigger a penalty APR

Frequently asked questions

Does asking for a lower interest rate hurt my credit?

Generally no. Asking your current issuer for a lower APR typically doesn't involve a hard credit inquiry. Confirm with the issuer if you're unsure.

How often can I ask for a lower rate?

There's no rule. Many people ask every six to twelve months, especially after their credit improves or they receive better offers.

What if I have bad credit?

It's still worth asking, especially about hardship programs. A nonprofit credit counselor may also be able to negotiate lower rates through a debt management plan.

Can I negotiate the interest rate on a car or student loan?

Installment loan rates are usually fixed in the contract. Refinancing with a new lender is the main way to get a lower rate on those loans.
