Loans: student, car, personal & home

Should I use a personal loan to pay off credit card debt?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: A personal loan can be a smart way to pay off credit cards if its APR (including any origination fee) is meaningfully lower than your cards, the term isn't much longer than you'd need otherwise, and you stop using the cards afterward. The benefit is a lower rate, one fixed payment and a firm payoff date. The risk is running the cards back up and ending with double the debt.

How it works

  1. You apply for an unsecured personal loan from a bank, credit union or online lender.
  2. If approved, you get a lump sum, or the lender pays your card issuers directly.
  3. You repay the loan in fixed monthly payments over a set term, often two to five years.
  4. Your card balances drop to zero.

Why a personal loan can beat credit cards

  • Lower APR for many borrowers with fair to good credit
  • Fixed rate and fixed payment, so no surprises when rates change
  • A firm end date. Credit cards have no payoff deadline, but loans do.
  • Lower credit utilization once cards are paid off, which may help your score. See credit utilization explained.

A worked example

Current situation: $12,000 across cards at about 23% APR, paying $400 a month. That's about 46 months and about $6,028 of interest.

Personal loan offer: 36 months at 12% APR with a 3% origination fee.

To get $12,000 to your cards after a 3% fee, you'd borrow about $12,371. The payment is about $410.89 a month.

How to compare personal loan offers

  • APR, which includes the origination fee
  • Term length: shorter means less total interest
  • Monthly payment: make sure it fits with room to save
  • Total of payments over the life of the loan
  • Fees: origination, late payment, and any prepayment penalty (many lenders have none)
  • Direct payment to creditors: some lenders offer it, which helps you avoid spending the money
  • Customer service and hardship options

Prequalify with several lenders. Many use a soft credit check that doesn't affect your score, then do a hard inquiry only when you formally apply.

When a personal loan is not a good idea

  • The APR isn't lower than your cards after fees.
  • The only appeal is a lower monthly payment from a much longer term.
  • You haven't fixed the spending that created the card debt.
  • Your debt is small enough to clear in a few months with a focused plan.
  • You'd qualify for a long 0% balance transfer that's cheaper. See balance transfer cards.

Protect yourself from the biggest risk

The biggest danger is running the cards back up. Zero balances can look like new money, and old habits return quickly when the pressure is off. Before you take the loan:

  1. Decide what happens to each card. Keep it open for credit history but remove it from your wallet, or lock it in the app.
  2. Set up a starter emergency fund so surprises don't land on a card. See how much emergency fund you need.
  3. Build a budget that includes the new loan payment. See zero-based budget.

Secured vs. unsecured personal loans

Most debt consolidation loans are unsecured, meaning no collateral. Some lenders, especially credit unions, offer secured personal loans backed by a savings account, certificate or vehicle. These can come with lower rates and easier approval, but you can lose the collateral if you default. If you're considering one, make sure the payment is comfortably affordable, and never pledge collateral you can't afford to lose. Ask the lender exactly what happens if you miss a payment.

Steps to do it right

  1. List your cards with balances and APRs.
  2. Check your credit reports for errors. See check your credit report.
  3. Prequalify with three or more lenders, including a local credit union.
  4. Choose the offer with the lowest total cost at a payment you can sustain.
  5. Have the lender pay the cards directly, or pay them the same day the funds arrive.
  6. Confirm each card shows a $0 balance.
  7. Automate the loan payment, and consider paying extra to finish early. See extra payments on loans.

Frequently asked questions

Is it smart to get a personal loan to pay off credit cards?

It can be, if the APR including fees is lower, the term isn't stretched out, and you stop using the cards. It's not smart if you'll keep charging new purchases.

Will a personal loan hurt my credit score?

A hard inquiry and new account can lower your score slightly at first. Paying off cards often reduces utilization, which can help, and on-time loan payments build positive history.

What is a good APR for a personal loan?

It depends on your credit and the market. The key is whether the APR, including fees, is clearly lower than what you're paying on your cards now.

Can I pay off a personal loan early?

Many lenders allow it without penalties. Check the loan agreement for prepayment terms before you sign.
