Debt basics

Where do I start paying off debt when it feels overwhelming?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Start by listing every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment. Then pause new borrowing, save a small emergency cushion so surprises don't land on a credit card, and build a simple budget that frees up extra money. Keep paying every minimum and put all the extra toward one debt at a time, using either the smallest balance (snowball) or the highest interest rate (avalanche).

First, take a breath

If you're staring at several statements and a total that makes your stomach drop, you're not alone, and you're not stuck. Debt feels overwhelming mostly because it's vague. The first job is to turn a fog of worry into a clear list. You can do that in an evening.

When I began my own payoff journey, my total was $49,113.44. I didn't fix that in a day. I made a list, chose a first target and started recording every contribution, beginning with $2.68. Small, boring, consistent steps are how this works.

Step 1: Make the list

Write down every debt. For each one, record:

  • Who you owe (lender or card name)
  • Current balance
  • Interest rate (APR)
  • Minimum monthly payment
  • Due date

If you're not sure you've found everything, pull your free credit reports. Our guide on checking your credit report shows how. Don't forget medical bills, family loans, and buy now, pay later plans.

Step 2: Stop digging

You can't fill a hole while you're still digging it. For the next few months:

  • Take credit cards out of your wallet and delete them from shopping sites and apps.
  • Turn off "pay later" options at checkout.
  • Pause new loans or financing offers, even 0% ones.

You don't necessarily need to close accounts. Closing cards can affect your credit utilization (see should you close a card after paying it off). Just stop using them.

Step 3: Build a starter cushion

Without any savings, the next flat tire goes right back on a credit card. Many people start by saving a small, fast emergency fund (often around $1,000, or one month of bare-bones expenses) before attacking debt aggressively. Then they grow it later. Read more in how much emergency fund you need.

Step 4: Find your monthly margin

Your "margin" is what's left after essentials and minimum payments. Find it with a written budget:

  1. Add up your monthly take-home pay.
  2. List essential costs: housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance.
  3. List your minimum debt payments.
  4. Whatever is left is the most you can put toward debt, saving and everything else.

A zero-based budget gives every dollar a job. If the margin is thin, look at cutting expenses and adding side income.

Step 5: Pick one target

Pay every minimum on time. Then put all your extra money toward one debt until it's gone. Two proven ways to choose the target:

  • Debt snowball: smallest balance first. Quick wins keep you motivated. See the debt snowball method.
  • Debt avalanche: highest interest rate first. It saves the most money. See the debt avalanche method.

With the list above and $500 a month total to put toward debt, the snowball would clear everything in about 28 months and the avalanche in about 27 months. The avalanche saves roughly $660 in interest, while the snowball wipes out the first debt within about four months. Both work. The best method is the one you'll stick with. Compare them in snowball vs. avalanche.

Step 6: Automate and track

  • Set every minimum payment on autopay so you never miss one. Late payments hurt your credit and can trigger penalty rates.
  • Schedule your extra payment right after payday, before the money gets spent.
  • Track your progress somewhere you'll see it. You can use the free, private debt tracker, which stores everything in your own browser.

Step 7: Roll payments forward

When the first debt is paid off, take its whole payment (minimum plus extra) and add it to the next debt's minimum. That growing payment is what makes debt payoff speed up over time.

Common first-month mistakes

  • Trying to do everything at once. One target at a time beats spreading extra dollars thinly.
  • Skipping the cushion. Without savings, one surprise can undo months of progress.
  • Not telling your household. A plan works far better when everyone knows it.
  • Waiting for a perfect month. Start with whatever extra you have, even $10.
  • Ignoring the due dates. Line up due dates with your paydays. Most lenders will move a due date if you ask, which makes it much easier to pay on time.

What progress looks like after 90 days

After three months of following these steps, you should have a complete list, a small cushion in savings, a budget you've used for a few pay cycles, and at least one balance visibly smaller. That's a real foundation. Debt payoff is mostly repetition from here: budget, pay, track, roll forward.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay off debt or save first?

Most people do best with a small starter emergency fund first, then aggressive debt payoff, then a fuller emergency fund. That order keeps surprises off your credit cards while you pay down high-interest debt.

Which debt should I pay off first?

Pay every minimum, then send extra money to one debt. Choose the smallest balance for motivation (snowball) or the highest interest rate to save the most money (avalanche).

How long will it take to pay off my debt?

It depends on your balances, rates and how much extra you can pay. List your debts and use a payoff calculator to see a month-by-month timeline.

What if I can't afford my minimum payments?

Call your lenders before you miss a payment and ask about hardship programs. Consider a nonprofit credit counselor, who can review options such as a debt management plan.
