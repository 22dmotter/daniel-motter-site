Credit scores

Credit utilization explained: what it is and how to lower it

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Credit utilization is the share of your available revolving credit you're using: your credit card balances divided by your credit limits. If you owe $3,000 on cards with $10,000 of total limits, your utilization is 30%. Lower utilization generally helps your credit score, and many people aim to stay under 30%, with lower being better. You can lower it by paying balances down, paying before your statement closes, and keeping paid-off cards open.

How to calculate credit utilization

Overall utilization = total card balances ÷ total card limits × 100.

Per-card utilization = one card's balance ÷ that card's limit × 100.

Example

CardBalanceLimitPer-card utilization
Card A$1,800$2,00090%
Card B$1,200$5,00024%
Card C$0$3,0000%
Total$3,000$10,00030% overall

Scoring models can look at both the overall number and individual cards. In this example, Card A is nearly maxed out even though overall utilization is 30%. Paying Card A down first can help.

Why utilization matters

Utilization is a major part of the amounts owed category, which FICO says makes up about 30% of its scores for the general population. High utilization can signal that you're relying heavily on credit. See how credit scores work.

What is a good utilization ratio?

There's no single magic number, but common guidance is:

  • Under 30% is a widely used rule of thumb
  • Under 10% is common among people with very high scores
  • 0% on every card is fine, though some models may favor showing small, regular use over no activity at all

The most important point: lower is generally better, and the effect is based on what's reported, not what you pay later.

Utilization has no long memory

Unlike late payments, which can stay on your report for years, utilization in most widely used scoring models is based on your current reported balances. If your utilization is high this month and low next month, your score can recover quickly. Some newer models consider balance trends over time, but paying down balances still helps.

When your balance gets reported

Card issuers usually report your statement balance to the credit bureaus around your statement closing date, not your payment due date. So even if you pay in full every month, a high statement balance can show high utilization.

Fix: Pay down part or all of the balance before the statement closes. You can make more than one payment a month.

7 ways to lower credit utilization

1. Pay down balances

The most direct method. Focus on cards with the highest per-card utilization, or the highest APR if you're also saving interest. See how to pay off credit card debt.

2. Pay before the statement date

Make a payment a few days before the statement closes so a lower balance gets reported.

3. Make multiple payments a month

Paying weekly or every payday keeps balances lower throughout the month.

4. Keep paid-off cards open

Closing a card removes its limit and can raise overall utilization. Keep no-fee cards open and use them lightly. See should I close a credit card after paying it off.

5. Ask for a credit limit increase

A higher limit lowers utilization if your spending stays the same. Ask whether the request requires a hard inquiry. This only helps if you don't spend more.

6. Spread spending carefully

Using one card heavily can make that card's utilization high. If you use cards for convenience, you can spread charges, but only if you pay them in full.

7. Consider a consolidation loan carefully

Moving card balances to an installment loan can lower revolving utilization. It only helps long term if you don't run the cards back up. See debt consolidation explained.

A worked paydown example

Using the table above, say you have $600 extra this month. If you put it all on Card A, its balance drops to $1,200, so per-card utilization falls from 90% to 60%, and overall utilization falls from 30% to 24%. Next month, another $600 brings Card A to 30% and overall utilization to 18%. Small, steady payments move both numbers quickly.

What doesn't count

  • Installment loans like car loans, mortgages and student loans aren't part of revolving utilization, though their balances matter in other ways
  • Debit cards don't affect credit
  • Charge cards without a preset spending limit may be treated differently by different scoring models

Utilization and paying off debt

Paying off cards is one of the fastest ways to see a score improve. As you work through your payoff plan, track your overall and per-card utilization each month. It's encouraging to watch it fall alongside your balances. Use our debt tracker to record balances privately in your browser.

Common mistakes

  • Closing old cards right after payoff, which can raise utilization
  • Maxing out one card while others sit empty
  • Waiting for the due date to pay when you want a low reported balance
  • Requesting a higher limit, then spending more

Frequently asked questions

What is a good credit utilization ratio?

Lower is generally better. Many people aim to stay under 30%, and people with very high scores often keep it under 10%.

Does credit utilization include loans?

Revolving utilization mainly includes credit cards and lines of credit. Installment loans like auto and student loans aren't part of that ratio, though their balances can matter elsewhere in your score.

How quickly does lowering utilization improve my score?

Often within one or two billing cycles, once your issuer reports the lower balance. In most widely used scoring models, utilization reflects your current reported balances.

Is 0% credit utilization bad?

Not bad, but some scoring models may slightly favor showing a small balance that you pay in full. You never need to carry a balance or pay interest to build credit.
