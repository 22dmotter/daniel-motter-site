Payoff strategies

Should I pay off debt or invest? A clear way to decide

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Compare your debt's interest rate with the return you could reasonably expect from investing, then adjust for risk. Paying off a 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% return, which beats what investments can reliably promise, so high-interest debt usually comes first. Low-rate debt (like a fixed-rate mortgage) is less urgent, and many people invest at the same time, especially to capture an employer retirement match.

The core idea: guaranteed vs. uncertain returns

When you pay off a debt, you "earn" its interest rate, because you stop paying that interest. That return is guaranteed: once the balance is gone, so is the interest.

Investments like stocks have historically grown over long periods, but their returns are not guaranteed and can be negative for years at a time. So the comparison isn't "22% vs. 7%." It's "a guaranteed 22% vs. an uncertain return that could be higher or lower."

A decision framework

1. Get any employer match first

If your employer matches retirement contributions, the match is often an immediate return of 25%, 50% or even 100% on the money you put in, up to a limit. Few debts cost more than that. Many people contribute enough to get the full match even while paying off debt. Check your plan's rules and vesting schedule.

2. Pay off high-interest debt next

Credit cards, store cards and payday loans usually charge rates far above what investments can reliably earn. Paying them off is typically the best "investment" available.

3. For middle-rate debt, weigh your priorities

For debts in the middle range, such as some car loans or private student loans, either choice can be reasonable. Many people split their extra money, part to debt and part to investing.

4. Low-rate debt can coexist with investing

A low, fixed-rate mortgage or low-rate federal student loan may cost less than long-term investment returns could be. Many people invest for retirement while paying these on schedule. Others pay them off early for peace of mind. Both are valid. See should I pay off my mortgage early.

Numbers that make it concrete

Paying debt: $5,000 on a card at 24% APR costs about $100 a month in interest at first ($5,000 × 24% ÷ 12). Paying it off saves that money every month, with no risk.

Investing: $5,000 invested at an assumed 7% average annual return grows to about $9,836 in 10 years and about $38,061 in 30 years. But real returns vary year to year and are not guaranteed.

A useful shortcut is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by an interest rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 7%, about 10.3 years. A 22% debt doubles in about 3.3 years if you don't pay it down. Debt compounds against you too, and fast. See what is compound interest.

Factors beyond the math

  • Risk tolerance: If market swings would cause you to panic and sell, paying off debt offers a calmer, certain return.
  • Liquidity: Money sent to debt is gone. You can't easily get it back in an emergency. Keep an emergency fund either way.
  • Taxes: Retirement accounts may offer tax advantages, and some interest, like student loan interest, may be tax-deductible within limits. Rules change, so check current IRS guidance or ask a tax professional.
  • Time horizon: The younger you are, the more years investments have to compound, which is one reason not to ignore retirement entirely.
  • Peace of mind: Being debt-free has real value that doesn't show up in a spreadsheet.

A sample plan

Jordan, 28, has a 401(k) with a match up to 4% of pay, $9,000 in card debt at 21% and a car loan at 6%:

  1. Keep a $1,000 starter emergency fund.
  2. Contribute 4% to get the full employer match.
  3. Send all other extra money to the credit card using the avalanche.
  4. After the card is gone, build three to six months of savings.
  5. Then increase retirement contributions while paying the car loan, or pay the car off early if being debt-free matters more.

What about other goals?

Debt and investing aren't the only places your money can go. Giving, saving for a home, and helping family are real priorities too. A written plan that names each goal and assigns it a monthly amount keeps you from bouncing between priorities every time you read a new article. Revisit the plan once or twice a year, or whenever your income or interest rates change significantly.

Common mistakes

  • Investing while carrying high-interest card balances because returns "might" be higher
  • Skipping a match to pay off low-rate debt faster
  • Cashing out retirement accounts to pay debt, which can trigger taxes and penalties and lose future growth
  • Having no emergency fund, so both plans get derailed by the first surprise

Frequently asked questions

Should I stop investing to pay off credit card debt?

Many people pause investing beyond any employer match while they pay off high-interest cards, because the guaranteed savings usually beat expected returns. Resume once the cards are gone.

Is it better to invest or pay off a low-interest loan?

For low fixed-rate debt, many people invest for retirement and pay the loan on schedule. Others prefer the security of paying it off early. Consider your risk tolerance, timeline and emergency savings.

Should I use my 401(k) to pay off debt?

Withdrawals or loans from retirement accounts can carry taxes, penalties and lost growth, and a 401(k) loan may come due if you leave your job. Treat it as a last resort and get professional advice first.

What interest rate is worth paying off before investing?

There's no universal cutoff, but many people prioritize paying off debt with rates above what they'd realistically expect from investments, especially credit cards.
