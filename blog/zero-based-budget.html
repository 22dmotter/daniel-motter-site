Budgeting & saving

How to make a zero-based budget: a step-by-step guide with an example

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: A zero-based budget gives every dollar of your monthly income a job until income minus planned spending, saving and debt payments equals zero. It doesn't mean spending everything; savings and extra debt payments are part of the plan. You list your take-home pay, cover essentials first, assign money to debts and savings, plan flexible spending, and adjust until the total is exactly zero. Then you track spending and adjust during the month.

How a zero-based budget works

Income − (spending + saving + debt payments) = $0

Every dollar is assigned before the month starts. If $200 is left over, you decide where it goes, such as extra debt payments or your emergency fund, instead of letting it disappear.

Step-by-step: build your zero-based budget

Step 1: Write down your monthly take-home pay

Use after-tax income: the money that actually hits your account. If your income varies, use a conservative estimate, such as your lowest month in the last six to twelve months.

Step 2: List the Four Walls first

Cover the essentials before anything else:

  1. Food (groceries)
  2. Shelter (rent or mortgage, utilities)
  3. Transportation (gas, insurance, transit, car payment)
  4. Basic clothing and health needs

Step 3: List minimum debt payments

Every debt's required minimum, so nothing goes late.

Step 4: Add savings and extra debt payments

Decide how much goes to your emergency fund, sinking funds for irregular expenses, and extra payments on your target debt. See sinking funds.

Step 5: Add flexible spending

Phone, subscriptions, personal spending, eating out, entertainment, gifts and giving.

Step 6: Make it zero

Add everything up. If you're over, cut flexible categories or find more income. If you're under, assign the remainder. When income minus outflows equals $0, your budget is done.

Step 7: Track and adjust

Record spending during the month. If you overspend in one category, move money from another so the budget still balances. That's not failure; it's how a zero-based budget is supposed to work.

Why zero-based budgeting helps get out of debt

  • No money goes unnoticed. Small leaks become visible.
  • Extra debt payments are planned, not leftover.
  • It creates intention. You decide what matters before the month starts.
  • It pairs well with the debt snowball or avalanche. The budget tells you how much extra you have each month to throw at your target debt.

Zero-based budgeting with irregular income

If you're paid on commission, freelance or tips:

  1. Budget on your lowest typical month.
  2. Rank your categories in priority order: essentials, minimum payments, then savings and extras.
  3. When income comes in, fund categories in order.
  4. Put surplus toward a buffer that smooths out lean months, then extra debt payments.
  5. Consider budgeting last month's income once you've built a one-month buffer.

Tips for sticking to it

  • Budget before the month begins, and do a quick weekly check-in
  • Plan for irregular expenses with sinking funds so they don't wreck the budget
  • Give yourself some fun money. A budget that's too strict is hard to keep.
  • Use cash envelopes or separate accounts for categories you tend to overspend
  • Budget with your partner if you share finances. Agree on the plan together.
  • Expect the first three months to feel messy. Budgets get easier as your estimates improve.

What to do with money left at the end of the month

If you underspend in a category, you have three good choices: roll it into the same category next month, move it to an emergency fund or sinking fund, or send it to your target debt. Decide in advance which one you'll use so leftover money doesn't quietly turn into extra spending. Many people sweep any leftover grocery or personal money straight to debt on the last day of the month.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting annual or quarterly bills, such as car registration and insurance premiums
  • Using gross income instead of take-home pay
  • Not leaving any room for personal spending
  • Giving up after one bad month
  • Not tracking spending against the plan

Frequently asked questions

What is a zero-based budget?

A budget where you assign every dollar of income to spending, saving or debt payments until income minus those amounts equals zero.

Does a zero-based budget mean I spend all my money?

No. Savings, investing and extra debt payments are categories in the budget. Zero means every dollar has a planned job, not that it's all spent.

How do I do a zero-based budget with irregular income?

Budget on your lowest typical month, rank categories by priority, fund them in order as money comes in, and build a buffer to smooth out lean months.

What app should I use for zero-based budgeting?

Any tool that lets you assign income to categories and track spending works, including a spreadsheet or paper. The best tool is the one you'll keep using.
