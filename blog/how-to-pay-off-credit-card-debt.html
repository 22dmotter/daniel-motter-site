Interest & credit cards

How to pay off credit card debt: a step-by-step plan that works

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: To pay off credit card debt, stop adding new charges, list every card's balance and APR, and pay every minimum on time. Then send all extra money to one card at a time, either the smallest balance (snowball) or the highest APR (avalanche), while lowering your rates through negotiation, a 0% balance transfer or a cheaper loan. A fixed payment well above the minimum is the biggest lever: $2,500 at 25% APR takes about 36 months at $100 a month, but only about 12 months at $250.

Why credit card debt is so hard to escape

Credit cards combine three things that work against you: high APRs, daily compounding and low minimum payments. Together, they keep balances alive for years. You're not bad at math or weak. The product is designed to be easy to carry and slow to pay off. A plan beats willpower.

Step 1: Stop the balance from growing

  • Stop using the cards, at least for now. Remove them from your wallet, phone and shopping accounts.
  • Switch everyday spending to a debit card or cash.
  • Cancel or move recurring subscriptions billed to the cards.
  • Don't close the accounts yet. That can affect your credit utilization. See should you close a card after paying it off.

Step 2: Gather your numbers

For each card, write down the balance, APR (check for promotional rates and their end dates), minimum payment and due date.

Step 3: Build a small cash buffer

Keep a small emergency fund so the next surprise doesn't land on a card. Many people start around $1,000 or one month of essential expenses. See pay off debt or save first.

Step 4: Choose your method

  • Debt avalanche (highest APR first) saves the most interest. In the example above: store card, then Card A, then Card B.
  • Debt snowball (smallest balance first) gives quick wins. Here the store card happens to be both smallest and highest-rate, so it goes first either way.

Compare them in snowball vs. avalanche.

Step 5: Set a fixed payment and stick to it

Minimums shrink as balances fall, which stretches payoff out for years. Choose a fixed monthly amount and keep paying it.

$2,500 at 25% APR, no new charges:

Fixed monthly paymentTime to pay offTotal interest
$100About 36 monthsAbout $1,068
$250About 12 monthsAbout $333

An extra $150 a month cuts payoff by two years and saves about $735. Find your number with the payment calculator.

Step 6: Lower your interest rate

Every point of APR you cut sends more of your payment to principal.

  1. Call and ask. Many issuers will lower your APR, especially for long-time customers who pay on time. Use the script in how to negotiate a lower interest rate.
  2. Use a 0% balance transfer, if your credit qualifies, and pay it off before the promotion ends. See balance transfer cards explained.
  3. Consolidate with a personal loan at a lower fixed rate. See using a personal loan to pay off credit cards.
  4. Consider a nonprofit debt management plan if your rates are high and your credit makes other options hard. See credit counseling and debt management plans.

Step 7: Find extra money

  • Trim big expenses first: housing, transportation, food. See cutting expenses.
  • Add income with extra shifts or freelance work. See side income ideas.
  • Redirect windfalls like tax refunds and bonuses.
  • Sell things you don't use, and send the money straight to your target card.

Step 8: Pay on or before the statement date

Paying before the statement closes can lower your reported balance, which helps your credit utilization. Paying early in the cycle can also slightly reduce interest, since cards charge on the average daily balance. See how credit card interest works.

Step 9: Roll payments forward

When a card hits zero, take its entire payment and add it to the next card. Payments grow as you go, so the last cards fall fastest.

Step 10: Stay out once you're out

  • Pay the full statement balance every month, or don't use the card.
  • Keep an emergency fund and sinking funds for predictable expenses.
  • Keep old, no-fee cards open if you can use them responsibly. They help your credit history and utilization.
  • See how to stay out of debt for long-term habits.

What about debt settlement or bankruptcy?

If your card debt is far beyond what you can repay, settlement or bankruptcy may come up. Both have serious consequences. Understand them before acting: debt settlement vs. bankruptcy. Talk with a nonprofit credit counselor or a licensed attorney first.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to pay off credit card debt?

Stop new charges, lower your APR if possible, and pay a fixed amount well above the minimum toward one card at a time. Use windfalls and extra income to speed it up.

Should I pay off the card with the highest interest or the lowest balance?

Highest interest first (avalanche) saves the most money. Lowest balance first (snowball) gives faster wins. Both work if you stay consistent.

Does paying off credit cards raise your credit score?

Often yes, because lower balances reduce your credit utilization, a major scoring factor. On-time payments also help. See our guide on whether paying off debt raises your score.

Is it better to pay off a credit card in full or leave a small balance?

Pay in full. You don't need to carry a balance or pay interest to build credit. On-time payments and low reported balances help your score.

Should I close my credit cards after paying them off?

Not necessarily. Closing cards can raise your utilization and eventually shorten your credit history. If a card has no annual fee and you won't be tempted, keeping it open is often better for your score.
