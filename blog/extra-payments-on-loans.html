How do extra payments on a loan work, and how much do they save?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: On most installment loans, interest is charged on the remaining principal, so any extra payment that goes directly to principal lowers future interest and shortens the loan. For example, adding $100 a month to a $20,000, 60-month car loan at 8% cuts the term by about 13 months and saves about $1,036 in interest. Always tell the lender to apply extra money to principal, and confirm there's no prepayment penalty.

How a loan payment is split

Most car loans, student loans, personal loans and mortgages are amortizing loans. Each payment covers that month's interest first, and the rest reduces your principal (the amount you still owe).

Early in a loan, a large share of each payment is interest. As the principal falls, the interest portion shrinks and more of each payment goes to principal. Extra payments speed up that shift.

Example: the first payment on a car loan

A $20,000 loan at 8% for 60 months has a payment of about $405.53.

  • First month's interest: $20,000 × 8% ÷ 12 = $133.33
  • Principal paid: $405.53 − $133.33 = $272.20

Add an extra $100 that month and principal falls by $372.20 instead. Next month's interest is charged on a smaller balance, and the savings compound from there.

Make sure extra money goes to principal

Lenders don't always apply extra money the way you'd expect. Some treat it as an early payment of next month's bill, which doesn't reduce principal right away and may even "advance" your due date. To avoid that:

  1. Look for a "principal only" option in your online account or payment portal.
  2. Include written instructions if you pay by check, such as "apply extra to principal."
  3. Check your next statement to confirm the principal dropped by the extra amount.
  4. Keep paying the regular amount every month. An advanced due date isn't a reason to skip a payment unless you've confirmed it won't count as late.

Federal student loans

For federal student loans, you can generally ask your servicer to apply extra payments to a specific loan, such as the highest-rate loan, and to not advance your due date. If you're pursuing forgiveness, like PSLF, or are on an income-driven plan, extra payments may not help you. Check before prepaying. See student loan forgiveness basics.

Watch for prepayment penalties

Most car, personal and federal student loans don't charge prepayment penalties, but some loans do. Look for a "prepayment" section in your loan agreement or ask the lender directly.

Also note that some loans, often subprime auto loans, use precomputed interest (sometimes called the "Rule of 78s") rather than simple interest. Paying early saves less on those. Ask how interest is calculated.

Biweekly payments: what they really do

Paying half your monthly payment every two weeks gives you 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 full payments instead of 12. That one extra payment per year is what shortens the loan. The schedule itself doesn't do anything magic.

You can get the same effect by:

  • Adding 1/12 of a payment to each monthly payment, or
  • Making one extra payment each year, for example from a tax refund

Avoid third-party biweekly services that charge enrollment or processing fees for something you can do yourself for free.

Where extra payments should go first

If you have several debts, extra money usually does the most good on the highest-interest balance (the avalanche method), or on the smallest balance if you want quick wins (the snowball method). A car loan at 6% generally comes after a credit card at 24%.

Should you shorten the term or lower the payment?

Some lenders let you "recast" a loan after a large extra payment, mostly mortgages. That keeps the payoff date the same but lowers your monthly payment. Others simply shorten the term. If you want to get out of debt faster, keep the payment the same and let the term shrink. If you need cash-flow relief, a recast may help.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to make one big extra payment or small monthly extras?

On a simple-interest loan, earlier principal reduction saves more interest, so a lump sum made now saves slightly more than the same amount spread over the year. Both are far better than no extra payments.

Do extra payments lower my monthly payment?

Usually not. Extra payments typically shorten the loan term while the monthly payment stays the same. Some loans, mainly mortgages, allow a recast that lowers the payment after a large prepayment.

Will paying off a loan early hurt my credit?

Paying off an installment loan can cause a small, temporary dip because an open account closes, but your payment history stays on your report and you save interest. For most people, the savings are worth it.

How do I know if my loan has a prepayment penalty?

Check the prepayment section of your loan agreement or ask the lender directly. Most car, personal and federal student loans don't have one, but some loans do.
