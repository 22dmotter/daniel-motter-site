Loans: student, car, personal & home

Student loan forgiveness basics: PSLF, IDR and other programs

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Federal student loan forgiveness is available through specific programs, not automatically. The main ones are Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF), after 120 qualifying monthly payments while working full time for a qualifying government or nonprofit employer, and forgiveness at the end of an income-driven repayment plan. There are also programs for teachers and discharges for disability, school closure and certain school misconduct. Private student loans generally aren't eligible. Check studentaid.gov for current rules.

Forgiveness vs. discharge vs. cancellation

You'll hear these words used interchangeably. Generally, forgiveness and cancellation relate to your job or repayment history, and discharge relates to circumstances like disability or a school closing. In every case, you no longer have to repay some or all of the loan. The terms matter less than whether you meet a program's specific rules.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)

PSLF forgives the remaining balance on Direct Loans after you make 120 qualifying monthly payments while working full time for a qualifying employer. According to Federal Student Aid, the basics are:

  • Qualifying employers include U.S. federal, state, local and tribal government organizations and certain nonprofits, such as 501(c)(3) organizations.
  • Qualifying loans are Direct Loans. Other federal loans may become eligible if consolidated into a Direct Consolidation Loan, but that has trade-offs. See repayment plans.
  • Qualifying payments must be made under a qualifying repayment plan, usually an income-driven plan.
  • The 120 payments don't have to be consecutive.

PSLF tips

  • Certify employment regularly using the PSLF Help Tool on studentaid.gov, not just at the end.
  • Keep records of employment and payment counts.
  • Don't refinance into a private loan if you're pursuing PSLF. You'd lose eligibility.
  • Extra payments usually don't help you on PSLF, since forgiveness is based on payment count and the remaining balance is forgiven.

Program rules, including which employers qualify, can change through new regulations. Always confirm with the official PSLF Help Tool.

Income-driven repayment (IDR) forgiveness

Income-driven plans tie your payment to your income. After you make payments for the full plan term, any remaining balance may be forgiven:

  • RAP: after 30 years of qualifying payments, according to studentaid.gov
  • IBR: after 20 or 25 years, depending on when you borrowed

IDR forgiveness can take decades. Whether forgiven amounts are taxed has changed over time and may depend on the year and the type of forgiveness. Check current IRS guidance or ask a tax professional.

Teacher Loan Forgiveness

Teachers who work five complete, consecutive academic years in a qualifying low-income school or educational service agency may be eligible for forgiveness of up to $17,500 on certain loans, depending on subject area, with a lower amount for others. Time used for Teacher Loan Forgiveness generally can't count toward PSLF at the same time, so compare which program helps more.

Discharges

  • Total and Permanent Disability (TPD) discharge for borrowers who meet disability criteria
  • Closed school discharge if your school closed while you were enrolled or soon after you withdrew
  • Borrower defense to repayment if your school misled you or engaged in certain misconduct
  • Death discharge for borrowers, and for Parent PLUS loans if the student dies
  • Bankruptcy discharge is possible but rare. It requires a separate legal proceeding showing undue hardship.

What about private student loans?

Private student loans aren't eligible for federal forgiveness programs. Some private lenders offer death or disability discharge, or temporary hardship options. Check your loan agreement and ask your lender.

A simple forgiveness checklist

  1. Log in to studentaid.gov and list every federal loan, its type and its servicer.
  2. Confirm whether each loan is a Direct Loan.
  3. Check whether your employer qualifies for PSLF using the PSLF Help Tool.
  4. Make sure you're on a qualifying repayment plan.
  5. Save copies of employment certifications and payment records.
  6. Review your payment count at least once a year.

How to avoid forgiveness scams

  • You never need to pay for help applying for federal forgiveness. Applications are free on studentaid.gov.
  • Never share your FSA ID password with anyone.
  • Be wary of anyone promising instant forgiveness or asking for upfront fees.
  • Communicate only with your official servicer and studentaid.gov.

Should you pay extra or aim for forgiveness?

This is one of the most important student loan decisions:

  • Pursuing PSLF with a stable public-service career: paying the required amount on a qualifying plan is often the best financial choice. Extra payments don't speed up PSLF.
  • Not eligible for forgiveness, or you expect to pay the loan off before IDR forgiveness: paying extra saves interest and shortens your debt. See pay off student loans faster.
  • Unsure: run your scenarios in the studentaid.gov Loan Simulator, and consider a fee-only advisor if the stakes are high.

Frequently asked questions

Who qualifies for Public Service Loan Forgiveness?

Borrowers with Direct Loans who make 120 qualifying payments under a qualifying plan while working full time for a qualifying government or nonprofit employer. Confirm with the PSLF Help Tool on studentaid.gov.

Are private student loans eligible for forgiveness?

No. Federal forgiveness programs only apply to federal student loans. Some private lenders offer death or disability discharge or hardship options.

Is student loan forgiveness taxable?

It depends on the program and current law. PSLF has generally been tax-free at the federal level, while tax treatment of other forgiveness has changed over time. Check current IRS guidance or a tax professional.

Should I pay a company to help with student loan forgiveness?

No. Applying for federal programs is free through studentaid.gov and your servicer. Avoid companies that charge upfront fees or ask for your FSA ID.
