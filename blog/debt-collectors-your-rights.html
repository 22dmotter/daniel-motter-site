Dealing with debt collectors: your rights and what to do

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Federal law, mainly the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), limits what third-party debt collectors can do. They can't harass you, lie, threaten arrest, or call at unusual times, generally before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your local time. You have the right to request validation of the debt, dispute it, and tell the collector to stop contacting you. Don't ignore a lawsuit; respond by the deadline. Report violations to the CFPB, the FTC or your state attorney general.

Who the FDCPA covers

The FDCPA mainly covers third-party debt collectors: collection agencies, debt buyers and attorneys who regularly collect debts for others. It generally doesn't cover original creditors collecting their own debts, though some state laws do. It applies to personal, family and household debts like credit cards, medical bills, car loans and mortgages, not business debts.

The CFPB's Regulation F adds detailed rules, including on calls, emails and texts.

What debt collectors can't do

Under federal law, collectors generally can't:

  • Call at inconvenient times, which are presumed to be before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your time
  • Call your workplace if they know your employer doesn't allow it
  • Harass you, such as by using threats, obscene language or repeated calls meant to annoy. Regulation F presumes harassment when a collector calls more than seven times in seven days about one debt, with some exceptions.
  • Lie, such as about the amount owed, being an attorney, or that you'll be arrested
  • Threaten actions they can't or don't intend to take
  • Discuss your debt with others, other than limited exceptions like your spouse or attorney. They can contact others only to find your contact information.
  • Add unauthorized fees or interest
  • Sue or threaten to sue over time-barred debts, which are debts past the statute of limitations

Your rights

Right to validation information

Collectors must give you a validation notice with the amount owed, the creditor's name, and how to dispute. This usually comes in the first communication or within five days after.

Right to dispute

If you dispute the debt in writing within the validation period, usually about 30 days after receiving the notice, the collector must stop collecting until it sends you verification.

Right to stop contact

You can tell a collector in writing to stop contacting you. They must stop, except to confirm they'll stop or to tell you about a specific action, such as a lawsuit. This doesn't erase the debt.

Right to choose how you're contacted

You can tell a collector which methods and times are inconvenient, and you can opt out of emails and texts.

What to do when a collector contacts you

1. Don't panic and don't pay on the spot

Get the collector's name, company, address and phone number. Don't give bank account information or make a payment until you've confirmed the debt is yours.

2. Request validation

Send a written request asking for the original creditor, the amount, and proof that the collector has the right to collect. Keep copies and consider certified mail.

3. Check your records and credit reports

Compare the debt with your records and your credit reports. See check your credit report.

4. Know the statute of limitations

Each state sets a time limit for suing over debts, often several years. In some states, a partial payment or written acknowledgment can restart the clock. The statute of limitations is separate from how long a debt can appear on your credit report, which is generally up to seven years from the original delinquency.

5. Decide your next step

If the debt is valid, you can:

  • Pay in full
  • Set up a payment plan you can actually afford
  • Negotiate a settlement for less than the full amount. Get the agreement in writing before paying, including that the balance will be considered resolved.
  • Seek help from a nonprofit credit counselor or attorney

Watch out for scams

Fake collectors may pressure you to pay immediately by gift card, wire or crypto. Real collectors must provide validation information. Look up the company independently and never pay a debt you can't verify.

If you're sued

Don't ignore it. If you don't respond to a lawsuit by the court's deadline, the collector can get a default judgment, which may allow wage garnishment or bank levies, subject to state law.

  1. Read the summons and note the deadline
  2. File a response with the court, even if you owe the debt
  3. Raise defenses such as the statute of limitations, wrong amount, or lack of proof
  4. Seek legal help. Legal aid organizations and some law school clinics help for free or low cost.

Some income, such as certain federal benefits, is generally protected from garnishment. Rules vary.

Where to report violations

  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau
  • Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
  • Your state attorney general

Under the FDCPA, you may also be able to sue a collector that violates the law, generally within one year of the violation. A consumer attorney can advise you.

Keep records

  • A log of every call: date, time, name and what was said
  • Copies of all letters, emails and texts
  • Proof of payments and any agreements

Take back control

Collections can feel overwhelming, but you have rights and options. Once you know what's valid, put each debt into a plan, whether that's the snowball, a payment plan, or legal help. See debt settlement vs. bankruptcy if your debts are beyond what you can repay.

Frequently asked questions

Can a debt collector call me at work?

Not if they know or have reason to know your employer prohibits it. Tell the collector your employer doesn't allow these calls, preferably in writing.

How do I make a debt collector stop calling?

Send a written request asking them to stop contacting you. They must stop, except to confirm or to notify you of specific actions like a lawsuit. The debt still exists.

Can a debt collector sue me for an old debt?

Not if the debt is past your state's statute of limitations; collectors can't sue or threaten to sue on time-barred debts. Be careful, since a payment can restart the clock in some states.

What should I do if a debt collector sues me?

Respond by the court's deadline, even if you owe the debt. Ignoring a lawsuit can lead to a default judgment and wage garnishment. Seek legal aid if you can.

Can debt collectors contact me by text or social media?

Under Regulation F, collectors can use texts, emails and social media private messages, but they must offer a simple way to opt out and can't post publicly about your debt.
