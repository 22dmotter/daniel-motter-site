Credit scores

Does paying off debt raise your credit score?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Usually, yes, especially for credit cards. Paying down card balances lowers your credit utilization, which is part of the amounts-owed category that FICO weighs at about 30% of its scores. Paying off an installment loan like a car loan may not raise your score much and can even cause a small temporary dip. Paying a collection may help with newer scoring models that ignore paid collections. Either way, being debt-free improves your finances.

It depends on the type of debt

Credit cards: usually the biggest boost

Credit cards are revolving debt. Scoring models look closely at your credit utilization: your balances divided by your credit limits. Paying balances down usually lowers utilization and can raise your score once the new balances are reported, often within a billing cycle or two.

Example: You have $8,000 of balances on $10,000 of total limits, which is 80% utilization. Paying it down to $2,000 drops utilization to 20%. Paying it to $0 drops it to 0%. See credit utilization explained.

Installment loans: smaller effect

For car loans, student loans, personal loans and mortgages, scores look at the account's history and how much you still owe. When you pay one off:

  • The account closes, but it can stay on your report for years as a positive closed account
  • Your credit mix might change if it was your only installment loan
  • Your score might dip slightly at first

That small dip isn't a reason to keep paying interest. The long-term benefits outweigh it.

Collections: depends on the model

Paying a collection doesn't automatically remove it from your report. However, some newer scoring models, including FICO Score 9 and 10 and VantageScore 3.0 and 4.0, ignore or treat paid collections differently than unpaid ones. Many lenders still use older models, so results vary. Medical collections have special rules. See medical debt: what to do.

Charged-off accounts

A charge-off stays on your report for up to seven years from the original delinquency, even if paid. Paying it updates the status to paid, which lenders may view more favorably, and it stops further collection activity.

Why your score might drop after paying off debt

It can be confusing to see your score dip after a big win. Common reasons:

  • Closing your only installment loan changed your credit mix
  • Closing a credit card reduced your total available credit, raising utilization on remaining cards
  • The account was your oldest, so closing it affected average age over time
  • Timing: a new statement reported a higher balance before your payoff posted

These effects are usually small and temporary.

How to get the most credit benefit from paying off debt

  1. Prioritize credit card balances if your goal includes your score. Paying them down helps utilization.
  2. Keep paid-off cards open if they have no annual fee. See should I close a credit card after paying it off.
  3. Pay before the statement closes to report a lower balance.
  4. Keep paying everything on time. Payment history is the biggest factor.
  5. Check your reports after payoff to make sure balances update correctly. See check your credit report.

Paying off debt vs. paying down debt

You don't have to reach zero to see credit benefits. Utilization is measured every time balances are reported, so each card you pay down helps. Many people see their scores rise steadily during a payoff plan, not just at the end. That's another reason to start now rather than waiting for a perfect plan or a big windfall.

Score vs. financial health

A credit score measures how you handle borrowing, not how wealthy or secure you are. Paying off debt:

  • Frees up monthly cash flow
  • Ends interest charges
  • Reduces stress and risk
  • Lets you save and invest more

Even if your score doesn't jump right away, your finances are stronger. A good score follows good habits over time.

What about credit repair companies?

You can do everything a credit repair company does for free: check your reports, dispute errors and build positive history. Under federal law, credit repair companies can't charge you before they perform services, and they can't remove accurate, timely negative information. Be wary of any company that promises a new credit identity or guaranteed results.

Frequently asked questions

How fast will my credit score go up after paying off a credit card?

Often within one or two billing cycles, once the card issuer reports the lower balance to the credit bureaus.

Why did my credit score go down after paying off a loan?

Paying off an installment loan can slightly change your credit mix and the number of active accounts. The dip is usually small and temporary.

Does paying off a collection raise my credit score?

It may with newer scoring models like FICO 9 and 10 and VantageScore 3.0 and 4.0, which treat paid collections differently. Older models may not change much.

Should I keep a small balance on my credit card to build credit?

No. You don't need to carry a balance or pay interest to build credit. Paying in full each month still builds positive payment history.
