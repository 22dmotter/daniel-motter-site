Loans: student, car, personal & home

Should I refinance my student loans? Pros, cons and the math

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Refinancing replaces your student loans with a new private loan, ideally at a lower interest rate. It can make sense for private student loans, or for federal loans if you have stable income, a strong credit profile, no plans to use forgiveness or income-driven repayment, and a meaningfully lower rate. Refinancing federal loans is permanent: you lose federal protections like income-driven plans, PSLF, deferment and forbearance options, and any future federal relief.

What student loan refinancing is

A private lender pays off your existing student loans and gives you one new private loan with a new rate, term and payment. It's different from a federal Direct Consolidation Loan, which keeps your loans federal but doesn't lower your rate. See debt consolidation explained.

The math: how much a lower rate saves

Say you owe $40,000 with 10 years left.

7%About $464.43About $15,732
5%About $424.26About $10,911

Cutting the rate from 7% to 5% saves about $40 a month and roughly $4,821 over 10 years, assuming the same term.

Watch the term

If you refinance into a longer term to lower the payment, you can pay more interest overall even at a lower rate. Compare total cost, not just the payment.

What federal borrowers give up

When you refinance federal loans into a private loan, you can't switch back. You give up:

  • Income-driven repayment plans such as RAP and IBR, where payments are tied to income. See student loan repayment plans.
  • Public Service Loan Forgiveness and IDR forgiveness. See student loan forgiveness basics.
  • Federal deferment and forbearance options
  • Federal discharge options, such as for school closure or certain disability situations, though some private lenders offer their own versions
  • Any future federal relief programs or payment pauses

For many borrowers, those protections are worth more than a modest rate cut, especially if your job or income is uncertain.

When refinancing can make sense

  • You have private student loans at a high rate. You're not giving up federal benefits.
  • You have federal loans, stable income, an emergency fund, and you're not pursuing forgiveness.
  • You'll get a meaningfully lower rate, not just a fraction of a percent.
  • You plan to pay the loans off aggressively anyway.

When to avoid refinancing

  • You work, or may work, in public service and could qualify for PSLF.
  • You rely on, or may need, income-driven payments.
  • Your income is unstable or your job is at risk.
  • The only benefit is a lower payment through a longer term.
  • You'd need a variable rate to get a lower rate and can't handle increases.

A quick self-test

Ask yourself these questions before applying:

  • If I lost my job for six months, could I keep making this payment?
  • Is there any chance I'll work for a government or nonprofit employer?
  • Is my new rate at least one or two percentage points lower?
  • Will I keep the same or a shorter repayment term?
  • Do I understand what happens if I can't pay a private lender?

If you hesitate on the first two questions, keeping federal loans federal is often the safer choice. Private lenders' hardship options are usually more limited and vary from lender to lender.

Fixed vs. variable rates

Fixed rates stay the same for the life of the loan. Variable rates can start lower but can rise with market rates. If you choose variable, have a plan to pay the loan off quickly, and make sure you could afford the payment if rates climb.

Checklist before you refinance

  1. List every loan: federal or private, rate, balance and servicer. Federal loans are listed on studentaid.gov.
  2. Decide whether you'd ever use IDR, PSLF or federal forbearance. If yes, keep those loans federal.
  3. Check your credit reports for errors. See check your credit report.
  4. Prequalify with several lenders using soft credit checks.
  5. Compare APR, term, fees, fixed vs. variable, and hardship policies.
  6. Consider refinancing only private loans, or only some federal loans, rather than all of them.
  7. Keep paying your current loans until the refinance is confirmed complete.

Who can qualify

Lenders typically look at your credit history, income, debt-to-income ratio and employment. Some allow a cosigner, which can help you qualify or get a lower rate, but the cosigner becomes equally responsible for the debt. If a cosigner is involved, ask whether the lender offers a cosigner release after a series of on-time payments.

A middle path: refinance some, not all

You don't have to refinance everything. Many borrowers refinance their highest-rate private loans while keeping federal loans federal. That captures savings where it's safest and keeps a safety net where it matters most.

Alternatives to refinancing

  • Extra payments on your highest-rate loan. See pay off student loans faster.
  • Autopay discounts, which many servicers and lenders offer as a small rate reduction
  • Employer student loan assistance, if your employer offers it
  • The avalanche method across your loans. See debt avalanche method.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a good idea to refinance federal student loans?

Only if you're confident you won't need federal protections like income-driven repayment, PSLF or federal forbearance, and the new rate is meaningfully lower. Refinancing federal loans into a private loan can't be undone.

Does refinancing student loans hurt your credit?

Applying usually involves a hard inquiry and a new account, which may lower your score slightly at first. Prequalification with a soft check doesn't affect your score.

Can I refinance student loans more than once?

Generally yes, if you qualify. Some borrowers refinance again when their credit or market rates improve. Compare fees and terms each time.

What is the difference between refinancing and consolidation?

Federal consolidation combines federal loans into one federal loan at a weighted-average rate and keeps federal benefits. Refinancing creates a new private loan that may lower your rate but ends federal benefits.
