What is the debt avalanche method and how much can it save?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: The debt avalanche is a payoff method where you pay the minimum on every debt, then put all extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate first. When that debt is paid off, you roll its payment into the next-highest rate. Because your most expensive debt shrinks first, the avalanche usually minimizes total interest and often gets you debt-free a little sooner.

How the debt avalanche works

  1. List your debts from highest APR to lowest, ignoring balances.
  2. Pay every minimum on time.
  3. Send every extra dollar to the highest-rate debt.
  4. When it's paid off, roll its full payment into the debt with the next-highest rate.
  5. Repeat until you're debt-free.

It's the same rolling-payment engine as the debt snowball. Only the order changes.

Why highest interest first saves money

Interest is the price you pay for carrying a balance. Each month, every debt charges interest on what you still owe. A dollar of extra payment on a 27% card stops more interest than a dollar on an 11% loan, so attacking the highest rate first reduces the total cost of your debt.

A rough way to see it: on a 27% APR card, $1,000 of balance costs about $22.50 a month in interest ($1,000 × 27% ÷ 12). On an 11% loan, the same $1,000 costs about $9.17. Pay down the 27% balance first and you save the difference every month that balance is gone. To understand how card interest builds, see how credit card interest works.

A worked example

Use the same three debts and $500 per month budget from our snowball guide:

DebtBalanceAPRMinimum
Card B$6,50027%$160
Card A$90018%$30
Personal loan$3,20011%$105

Minimums total $295, leaving $205 extra.

  • Months 1–24: Card B gets $160 + $205 = $365 a month and is paid off in month 24.
  • Meanwhile, Card A and the personal loan keep getting their minimums, which shrink them steadily.
  • Month 25: Card A's small remaining balance is cleared.
  • Month 27: The personal loan is paid off.

Total interest: about $2,666, with everything paid off in 27 months.

The snowball on the same debts takes about 28 months and costs about $3,328. The avalanche saves roughly $662 and a month of payments. The catch is that your first payoff doesn't happen until month 24.

These are simplified estimates that assume fixed rates and minimums and no new charges. Model your own debts in the payoff planner.

When the avalanche saves the most

The gap between methods grows when:

  • Your biggest balance has your highest rate, like a large credit card balance alongside small low-rate loans
  • Rates are spread far apart, such as 29% vs. 6%
  • Balances are large and payoff will take years

When rates are close together, the savings shrink and the snowball's motivation edge may matter more.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Lowest total interest in most situations
  • Often finishes sooner
  • Logical: it treats debt like the expense it is

Cons

  • The first win can take a long time if the highest-rate debt is large
  • Requires patience and tracking to stay motivated
  • If you lose steam and quit, the savings never happen

How to stay motivated on the avalanche

  • Track the balance, not just the accounts. Watching one total fall each month is its own win. The free debt tracker shows progress over time.
  • Celebrate milestones, such as every $1,000 paid off or each 10% of the big debt.
  • Lower the rate while you wait. Call the issuer to ask for a lower APR, or consider a balance transfer for the top-rate card.
  • Use a hybrid if needed. Knock out one tiny balance first for a quick win, then go back to highest rate first.

A quick avalanche checklist

  1. Gather your latest statement for every debt and write down the APR, balance and minimum.
  2. Sort from highest APR to lowest. Break ties by putting the smaller balance first.
  3. Add up the minimums and subtract them from what you can pay each month. That difference is your extra payment.
  4. Schedule the extra payment to the top debt right after payday.
  5. Each time a debt hits zero, add its full payment to the next debt on the list.
  6. Review the list every few months, or any time a rate changes.

Avalanche tips that make a real difference

  • Confirm every APR. Look at your latest statements. Promotional rates expire, and penalty APRs can kick in after late payments.
  • Watch for deferred-interest promotions. Some store cards charge all the back interest if the promo balance isn't paid in full by the deadline, so those can jump to the top of your list.
  • Re-sort when rates change. If a rate goes up or down, update your order.
  • Never skip minimums on the lower-priority debts. Late fees and credit damage would wipe out your savings.
  • Know your variable rates. Many credit cards and some private loans have variable APRs tied to a benchmark rate. If rates rise, the order of your list may change.

Frequently asked questions

Is the debt avalanche or snowball better?

The avalanche usually saves more money. The snowball often keeps people more motivated because of faster early payoffs. If you're confident you'll stick with it, the avalanche is the cheaper route.

Should a 0% promotional balance go last in the avalanche?

Usually, while the promotion lasts. But plan to pay it off before the promotion ends, and treat deferred-interest promotions as urgent, because back interest may be charged if a balance remains.

Do I include my mortgage in the avalanche?

Most people focus on consumer debts first and leave the mortgage out. Mortgage rates are typically lower than cards and personal loans anyway.

How do I find my interest rates?

Your APR is on each monthly statement and in your online account. For loans, check the loan agreement or call the lender.
