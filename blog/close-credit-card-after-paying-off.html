Interest & credit cards

Should I close a credit card after paying it off?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Often it's better to keep a paid-off, no-annual-fee card open and use it lightly or not at all, because closing it can raise your credit utilization and eventually shorten your credit history. Closing can make sense if the card has an annual fee you don't want to pay, if you can't trust yourself with it, or if you're simplifying your finances and the credit impact won't matter for your goals.

How closing a card can affect your credit

Credit utilization goes up

Credit utilization is the share of your available revolving credit you're using. It's a big part of the "amounts owed" category, which myFICO lists as about 30% of a FICO Score. Closing a card removes its limit from your total available credit.

Example:

Before closingAfter closing Card B
Card A limit$5,000$5,000
Card B limit$5,000Closed
Total limit$10,000$5,000
Balances$2,000$2,000
Utilization20%40%

Same debt, higher utilization, and potentially a lower score. If your other cards have no balances, the effect is smaller. See credit utilization explained.

Credit history length can shrink over time

Closed accounts in good standing generally stay on your credit report for years, so closing doesn't wipe out the history right away. Over time, though, once the account drops off, your average account age may fall. Length of credit history is about 15% of a FICO Score, according to myFICO.

Credit mix rarely changes

Closing one card usually won't change your credit mix much if you still have other cards or loans.

When keeping it open makes sense

  • It has no annual fee.
  • It's one of your oldest accounts.
  • It has a large credit limit that keeps your utilization low.
  • You can avoid using it, or use it for one small bill and pay it in full.
  • You plan to apply for a mortgage or car loan soon and want your score steady.

When closing it makes sense

  • It has an annual fee that isn't worth it. First ask whether you can switch to a no-fee version of the card.
  • You keep running it up. Protecting your progress is more important than a few score points.
  • You're simplifying your finances and don't need the credit.
  • The card has unfavorable terms you can't change.
  • You suspect fraud or unauthorized use. Contact the issuer about a new account number first.

Safer alternatives to closing

  1. Remove the card from your wallet and online stores so it's harder to use impulsively.
  2. Put one small recurring bill on it (like a streaming subscription) and set autopay to pay the full statement balance. This keeps it active so the issuer is less likely to close it for inactivity.
  3. Ask for a "product change" to a no-fee card from the same issuer, which can keep the account history.
  4. Lower the credit limit if a high limit tempts you, though this also raises utilization.
  5. Freeze it if your issuer's app lets you lock the card.

If you decide to close it

  • Pay the balance to zero and redeem any rewards first.
  • Close the right card. Usually a newer, lower-limit or fee-charging card rather than your oldest.
  • Close one at a time, especially if you're planning a big loan application.
  • Confirm in writing that the account is closed, and check your credit report later. See check your credit report and dispute errors.
  • Update any automatic payments that were linked to the card.

A quick decision checklist

Ask yourself these questions in order:

  1. Does the card charge an annual fee? If yes, ask for a no-fee product change. If that's not possible and the fee isn't worth it, closing is reasonable.
  2. Will I be tempted to use it? If you've run it up before, consider closing it or at least locking it in the issuer's app.
  3. Is it my oldest account or my biggest limit? If yes, keeping it open usually helps your score the most.
  4. Am I applying for a mortgage or car loan in the next year? If yes, avoid closing cards until after the loan closes.
  5. Do my other cards carry balances? If yes, closing will raise utilization more. Pay them down first.

There's no single right answer. Your decision should protect both your progress and your peace of mind.

Inactivity closures

Issuers can close cards that go unused for a long time. If a card matters to your credit, a small recurring charge paid in full keeps it active. Read your issuer's terms for specifics.

The bigger picture

Your credit score is a tool, not the goal. The goal is freedom: no balances, no interest and money working for you. If an open card puts that at risk, closing it may be the wiser choice, even if your score dips a bit. Payment history (about 35% of a FICO Score) and low balances will rebuild any temporary dip. Frequently asked questions

Does closing a credit card hurt your credit score?

It can, mainly by raising your credit utilization because your total available credit falls. The effect depends on your other balances and limits.

Does a closed account stay on my credit report?

Generally yes. Closed accounts in good standing typically remain on your report for years, and negative accounts generally remain for up to about seven years from the delinquency.

Should I close my oldest credit card?

Usually not, unless it has an annual fee you can't avoid or it's tempting you into debt. Older accounts help your credit history length.

Will my card be closed if I don't use it?

It might. Issuers can close inactive accounts. A small recurring charge that you pay in full can keep it active.
