Interest & credit cards

What happens if you only pay the minimum on your credit card?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: If you only pay the minimum, you stay current and avoid late fees, but most of your payment goes to interest and the balance falls very slowly. In one example, a $5,000 balance at 22% APR with a minimum of interest plus 1% of the balance would take about 19 years to pay off and cost about $8,100 in interest. Paying a fixed $200 a month instead clears it in about 34 months with about $1,750 of interest.

How minimum payments are calculated

Each issuer sets its own formula, listed in your cardholder agreement. Common versions are:

  • Interest + fees + 1% of the balance, or
  • A percentage of the balance (often 1% to 3%), and
  • A floor, such as $25 or $35, or the full balance if it's smaller

Because the minimum shrinks as your balance falls, your payment keeps getting smaller, and payoff drags on for years.

A worked example

Balance: $5,000. APR: 22%. Minimum: interest + 1% of the balance, at least $25.

First month:

  • Interest: $5,000 × 22% ÷ 12 = $91.67
  • 1% of balance: $50
  • Minimum payment: $141.67, of which only $50 reduces the balance

Over time, paying only the minimum:

ApproachTime to pay offTotal interest
Minimum only (shrinking payment)About 230 months (over 19 years)About $8,100
Fixed $200 per monthAbout 34 monthsAbout $1,750

Keeping your payment fixed at $200, just $58 more than the first minimum, saves roughly $6,350 and about 16 years. (Estimates assume a fixed APR and no new charges.)

Read the minimum payment warning on your statement

Under federal rules, card statements must include a minimum payment warning showing:

  • How long it would take to pay off the balance making only minimum payments
  • The total you'd pay that way
  • The monthly payment needed to pay it off in about three years, and the total cost at that payment

That three-year number is a great starting goal.

Why minimums are set so low

Low minimums keep accounts current and make cards feel affordable. But a low minimum also means you carry the balance longer, so you pay more interest. The minimum is the least you can pay to avoid a late payment, not a recommended payoff plan.

Is paying the minimum ever okay?

Yes, temporarily and strategically:

  • On non-target debts while you put extra money toward one debt at a time with the snowball or avalanche
  • During a short hardship, to stay current while you stabilize
  • While building a starter emergency fund

Always pay at least the minimum on time. Missing it can mean late fees, a penalty APR and damage to your credit.

How to escape the minimum payment trap

  1. Pick a fixed payment above the minimum and keep paying it even as the minimum falls.
  2. Stop new charges on the card.
  3. Lower the APR. Call and ask, consider a balance transfer, or a lower-rate personal loan.
  4. Find extra money in your budget. See cutting expenses and side income.
  5. Use the three-year number on your statement as your minimum goal.
  6. Automate it so the fixed payment happens every month.

What if you can't afford more than the minimum?

If minimums are all you can manage, focus on:

  • Protecting essentials and avoiding new debt
  • Calling issuers to ask for hardship programs or lower rates
  • Talking with a nonprofit credit counselor about a debt management plan

Even an extra $10 to $25 a month starts to break the cycle.

How the minimum changes over time

The minimum payment is recalculated every month based on the new balance. In the example above, the first minimum is about $141.67. As the balance falls, the minimum falls too. After a few years it's closer to $100, and eventually it reaches the $25 floor. Each drop feels like relief, but it actually means less of your money is fighting the balance. That's why paying a fixed amount, rather than whatever the statement asks for, is so effective. Your payment stays the same while the interest portion shrinks, so more and more of each payment goes to principal.

The emotional side of minimums

Minimum payments can create a false sense of control: everything is "current," nothing is late, and the statements keep coming. Many people don't realize they're stuck until they look at how much they've paid over a year compared with how little the balance moved. If that's you, don't beat yourself up. Pick a fixed payment, write it in your budget and start today.

Frequently asked questions

Does paying the minimum hurt my credit?

Paying the minimum on time counts as an on-time payment. But a high balance relative to your limit raises your credit utilization, which can lower your score.

How long does it take to pay off a credit card with minimum payments?

Often many years, depending on the balance, APR and the issuer's formula. Your statement shows the estimated time and total cost.

What is the minimum payment on a $1,000 credit card balance?

It depends on your issuer's formula. Many cards set a minimum around 1% to 3% of the balance plus interest, with a floor such as $25 or $35. Check your statement.

Is it better to pay the minimum on all cards or more on one?

Pay the minimum on all cards to stay current, then put any extra toward one card at a time. That focused approach pays debt off faster than spreading extra evenly.
