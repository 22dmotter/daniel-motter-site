Loans: student, car, personal & home

Upside down on your car loan? What it means and what to do

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Being upside down on a car loan means you owe more than the car is worth. That difference is called negative equity. If you owe $22,000 and the car is worth $17,500, you have $4,500 of negative equity. The safest fix is usually to keep the car and pay extra toward principal until the loan balance is below the car's value. Avoid rolling negative equity into a new loan, which starts the next loan even deeper underwater.

How to tell if you're upside down

  1. Find your payoff amount. Call your lender or check your online account. The payoff amount includes interest through a specific date and may differ from your statement balance.
  2. Estimate your car's value. Use pricing guides and real offers from dealers or online car buyers. A firm offer from a buyer is the most realistic number.
  3. Subtract. Payoff minus value equals negative equity, if the result is positive.

Example: Payoff of $22,000, value of $17,500. You're upside down by $4,500.

Why it happens

  • Fast depreciation. New cars often lose value quickly in the first few years.
  • Small or no down payment
  • Long loan terms such as 72 or 84 months, where payments shrink principal slowly
  • High interest rates
  • Rolling negative equity from a previous car into the new loan
  • Add-ons financed into the loan, such as extended warranties

Why it matters

Negative equity only becomes a real loss when you sell, trade in, or the car is totaled. If the car is totaled or stolen, insurance generally pays the car's actual cash value, not your loan balance, unless you have gap coverage. You'd still owe the difference.

Ways to get right-side up

1. Keep the car and pay extra

The cleanest solution. Every extra principal payment narrows the gap. On a loan near 9%, an extra $150 a month can shorten a 48-month loan by about 15 months. See should I pay off my car loan early.

2. Make a lump-sum payment

A tax refund or bonus aimed at principal can erase part or all of the negative equity.

3. Refinance to a lower rate, not a longer term

If your credit has improved, a lower rate means more of each payment goes to principal. Keep the term the same or shorter. Note that some lenders won't refinance a loan with significant negative equity.

4. Check gap coverage

If you don't have gap insurance and you're significantly upside down, ask your insurer about adding it. Compare prices; it's often cheaper through an insurer than a dealership. Some policies have eligibility limits.

5. Sell privately and cover the difference

If you must get out of the car, a private sale often brings more than a trade-in. You'd need to pay the lender the difference between the sale price and the payoff, either from savings or a small personal loan, before the title can transfer.

6. Keep it longer

Once the loan is paid off, driving the car for years without a payment is one of the best ways to build savings. See how to stay out of debt.

What not to do: roll it into the next loan

Dealers may offer to "pay off your trade" no matter how much you owe. What usually happens is the negative equity is added to your new loan.

Example: You buy a $22,000 car and roll in $4,500 of negative equity. You'd finance about $26,500, before taxes and fees, on a car worth $22,000 or less as soon as you drive away. That puts you even deeper underwater and can lead to a cycle of bigger loans. If you must trade in, try to pay the negative equity in cash instead.

How to avoid being upside down next time

  • Make a larger down payment, ideally 20% or more on a new car
  • Choose a shorter loan term, such as 48 months or less
  • Buy a reliable used car that has already taken its biggest depreciation hit
  • Don't finance add-ons you don't need
  • Get preapproved by a bank or credit union before visiting the dealer

If you can't afford the payment

  • Call the lender early. Ask about deferment, due-date changes or hardship options before you miss payments.
  • Consider selling the car and buying a cheaper one with cash or a small loan, if the numbers work.
  • Avoid voluntary surrender without understanding the cost. After repossession, the car is sold, and you can still owe the remaining balance plus fees.

Build it into your debt plan

Negative equity is just debt with a deadline you choose. List the car loan alongside your other debts and decide where it fits in your snowball or avalanche. Our calculator can show how extra payments change your payoff date.

Frequently asked questions

What does upside down on a car loan mean?

It means you owe more on the loan than the car is worth. The difference is called negative equity.

Can I trade in a car I'm upside down on?

Yes, but the negative equity is usually added to your new loan, which can leave you deeper underwater. Paying the difference in cash is safer.

How do I get out of an upside-down car loan?

The safest route is to keep the car and pay extra toward principal. Other options include a lump-sum payment, refinancing at a lower rate without extending the term, or selling privately and paying the difference.

Does gap insurance help if I'm upside down?

Gap coverage can pay the difference between your loan balance and the car's actual cash value if the car is totaled or stolen, subject to policy terms. It doesn't reduce what you owe otherwise.
