Debt basics

Good debt vs. bad debt: is any debt actually good?

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: "Good debt" usually means borrowing for something that can raise your income or net worth, at a low, fixed cost you can comfortably repay, like a reasonable mortgage or an affordable degree. "Bad debt" is high-interest borrowing for things that lose value or get used up, like credit card balances. In practice the label matters less than three questions: what does it cost, what does it buy, and can you repay it without strain?

Why people talk about "good" and "bad" debt

Almost every money conversation eventually splits debt into two buckets. Mortgages, student loans and business loans land in the "good" bucket. Credit cards, payday loans and financed vacations land in the "bad" one. The idea is that some borrowing is an investment and some is just spending money you don't have yet.

That framing has a kernel of truth, but it can also give people permission to borrow far more than is wise. A $120,000 degree that leads to a $40,000 job is not automatically "good" just because it's a student loan. And a car loan isn't automatically "bad" if it's small, low-rate and gets you to a better-paying job.

A better test: cost, purpose and capacity

Instead of asking which bucket a loan belongs to, ask three questions.

1. What does it cost?

Look at the APR (annual percentage rate), not just the monthly payment. APR includes interest and most fees, so it's the fairest way to compare loans. A 6% fixed-rate loan and a 29% credit card are completely different animals, even if both payments "fit" your budget. If you're fuzzy on the difference, read APR vs. interest rate.

2. What does it buy?

Does the thing you're financing tend to grow in value or raise your earning power (a home in a stable market, training for a specific in-demand job, equipment for a business that already has customers)? Or does it lose value quickly or get used up (clothes, dining out, electronics, most vehicles, vacations)? Borrowing for things that are gone before the bill is paid is how balances snowball.

3. Can you repay it comfortably?

Even "good" debt becomes bad when the payment crowds out savings, giving and basic needs. A useful check is your debt-to-income ratio: total monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. The lower, the more breathing room you have.

Worked example: two $10,000 decisions

Imagine two people who each borrow $10,000.

Person A takes a 5-year loan at 9% to finish a certification that qualifies them for a raise. The payment is about $207.58 per month, and the total interest over five years is about $2,455. If the raise is even $300 a month after tax, the loan pays for itself and then some.

Person B carries $10,000 on a credit card at 24% APR and pays $250 a month. The first month alone costs roughly $200 in interest ($10,000 × 24% ÷ 12), so only about $50 of that $250 actually reduces the balance. It will take years to clear and cost thousands in interest, with nothing left to show for the original purchases.

Same dollar amount, completely different outcome. The difference is cost, purpose and whether there's a realistic path to zero.

Where "good debt" goes wrong

  • Borrowing the maximum you're approved for. Lenders approve based on what you can pay, not what's wise. Buying the biggest house or car a bank allows often leaves no margin.
  • Assuming the payoff is guaranteed. Degrees, homes and businesses are bets, not certainties. Markets change and careers change.
  • Ignoring opportunity cost. Every dollar going to a payment is a dollar not going to your emergency fund, retirement or giving.
  • Stacking "good" loans. A mortgage, two car loans and student loans can each look reasonable alone and still add up to a payment you can't carry.

My take after living with debt

I started my own public payoff journey owing $49,113.44. Some of it came from choices that sounded like "good debt" at the time. What I've learned is that any debt is a claim on your future paycheck. It limits your options, and it adds stress you carry into your relationships and work. That's why I try to live by Romans 13:8: "Let no debt remain outstanding, except the continuing debt to love one another."

That doesn't mean you can never borrow. It means you borrow rarely, borrow small, borrow cheap, and always have a plan to get back to zero.

How to decide on your next loan

  1. Write down the full cost: APR, fees and total interest over the life of the loan.
  2. Ask whether the purchase can wait while you save part or all of it.
  3. Check your debt-to-income ratio with the new payment included.
  4. Make sure you can keep at least a starter emergency fund intact.
  5. Write the payoff plan before you sign. Use the payment calculator to see how long it really takes.

If you already have debt you regret, don't waste time on guilt. Start with where to begin paying off debt and pick a method like the debt snowball or debt avalanche.

Frequently asked questions

Is a mortgage good debt?

A mortgage can be reasonable debt when the payment fits comfortably, the rate is fixed and fair, and you plan to stay long enough to come out ahead of buying and selling costs. It becomes risky when you stretch to the maximum approval or have no emergency savings.

Are student loans good debt?

They can be, if the program reliably leads to higher earnings and you borrow modestly compared with your expected starting salary. They are among the hardest debts to escape, so borrow only what you truly need.

Is credit card debt ever good?

Using a credit card and paying the full statement balance every month is not really debt. Carrying a balance at a high APR is almost always harmful because interest compounds quickly on purchases that rarely hold value.

Should I pay off good debt early?

Pay off high-interest debt first. For low-rate debt, paying early is a personal choice that buys freedom and lowers risk. Many people prefer it, especially once they have an emergency fund and are saving for retirement.
