Credit scores

How to check your credit report for free and dispute errors

By Daniel Motter · Nov 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: You can get free credit reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion at AnnualCreditReport.com, the official site authorized by federal law, and the bureaus now offer free reports weekly. Review each report for accounts you don't recognize, wrong balances, incorrect late payments and old negative items. If you find an error, dispute it with the bureau reporting it and, when helpful, with the company that supplied the information. Disputes are free.

Where to get your free credit reports

Use AnnualCreditReport.com. It's the official source for free reports from all three nationwide bureaus. Be cautious of look-alike sites that require a credit card or push paid subscriptions.

You can check all three at once or spread them out during the year. Each bureau may have slightly different information, so checking all three is the most thorough.

Checking your own report doesn't hurt your credit score.

What's on a credit report

  • Personal information: name, addresses, Social Security number fragments, employers
  • Accounts: credit cards, loans, mortgages, with balances, limits and payment history
  • Public records: in particular, bankruptcies
  • Collections
  • Inquiries: hard inquiries from applications and soft inquiries

Your report doesn't include your credit score, though many services show scores separately.

What to look for

Go line by line. Common errors include:

  • Accounts that aren't yours, possibly from identity theft or mixed files
  • Wrong personal information, such as a misspelled name or unknown address
  • Incorrect late payments
  • Wrong balances or credit limits
  • Closed accounts reported as open, or accounts you closed showing as closed by the creditor
  • Duplicate accounts, such as the same debt listed by the original creditor and a collector as both owed
  • Negative items older than the reporting limit. Most negative information, including late payments and collections, can generally be reported for up to seven years, and some bankruptcies for up to ten.

How to dispute an error

Step 1: Gather evidence

Collect statements, payment confirmations, letters or anything else showing the information is wrong. Make copies; keep originals.

Step 2: Dispute with the credit bureau

File a dispute with each bureau showing the error, online, by mail or by phone. Explain clearly:

  • Which item is wrong
  • Why it's wrong
  • What correction you want

Attach your supporting documents. If you mail it, consider certified mail with a return receipt.

Step 3: Dispute with the company that reported it

You can also dispute directly with the furnisher, the lender or collector that supplied the information. This can be helpful because the furnisher has the original records.

Step 4: Wait for the investigation

Under federal law, bureaus generally must investigate disputes, usually within 30 days, and forward your information to the furnisher. You'll receive the results in writing. If information is corrected or deleted, you can ask the bureau to send the updated report to anyone who requested it recently.

Step 5: If the dispute is rejected

  • Ask what the furnisher verified and how
  • Submit additional evidence
  • Add a brief statement of dispute to your file
  • File a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

Sample dispute letter outline

  1. Your full name, address and date of birth
  2. Your report or confirmation number, if any
  3. The item you're disputing: creditor name and account number, partially masked
  4. A short explanation: "This account was paid in full on [date] and should show a $0 balance."
  5. The correction you request
  6. A list of enclosed documents

Keep it factual and short.

If you suspect identity theft

  • Place a fraud alert with one bureau, which notifies the other two. Fraud alerts are free.
  • Freeze your credit at all three bureaus. Freezes are free and stop most new accounts from being opened in your name. You can lift them temporarily when you apply for credit.
  • Report it at IdentityTheft.gov, which creates a recovery plan.
  • Dispute fraudulent accounts with the bureaus and companies.

Accurate negative information

If a late payment or collection is accurate, the bureaus generally don't have to remove it. You can still:

  • Ask the creditor for a goodwill adjustment on a one-time late payment, explaining the circumstances. They aren't required to agree.
  • Focus on building positive history. Negative items lose impact over time.

Avoid credit repair scams

Legitimate errors can be disputed for free. Under federal law, credit repair companies can't charge before performing services and can't legally remove accurate, timely information. Avoid anyone who:

  • Demands payment upfront
  • Tells you to dispute accurate information
  • Suggests creating a "new credit identity"
  • Guarantees a specific score increase

How often to check

A good routine is to check at least one report every few months and all three before a major application like a mortgage or car loan. Because free weekly reports are available, you can also check after paying off an account to confirm it updated.

Make it a habit

Set a reminder to check your reports regularly, especially before applying for a loan and while paying down debt. Confirm that paid-off accounts show $0 balances and correct statuses. It's a simple way to make sure your progress shows up.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I get my credit report for free?

At AnnualCreditReport.com, the official site for free reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. The bureaus now offer free reports weekly.

Does checking my credit report lower my score?

No. Checking your own credit report is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect your score.

How long does a credit report dispute take?

Credit bureaus generally must investigate within about 30 days, and they'll send you the results in writing.

Can I remove accurate negative items from my credit report?

Generally no. Accurate, timely information can be reported for the legal time limits, typically up to seven years for most negative items. You can request a goodwill adjustment, but creditors don't have to agree.
